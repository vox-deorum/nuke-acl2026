# SmartPlay: A Benchmark for LLMs as Intelligent Agents (Wu et al. 2024) [wu2024smartplay]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01557
https://openreview.net/forum?id=S2oTVrlcp3

Abstract: Recent large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated great potential toward intelligent agents and next-gen automation, but there currently lacks a systematic benchmark for evaluating LLMs' abilities as agents. We introduce SmartPlay: both a challenging benchmark and a methodology for evaluating LLMs as agents. SmartPlay consists of 6 different games, including Rock-Paper-Scissors, Tower of Hanoi, Minecraft. Each game features a unique setting, providing up to 20 evaluation settings and infinite environment variations. Each game in SmartPlay uniquely challenges a subset of 9 important capabilities of an intelligent LLM agent, including reasoning with object dependencies, planning ahead, spatial reasoning, learning from history, and understanding randomness. The distinction between the set of capabilities each game test allows us to analyze each capability separately. SmartPlay serves not only as a rigorous testing ground for evaluating the overall performance of LLM agents but also as a road-map for identifying gaps in current methodologies. We release our benchmark at https://github.com/microsoft/SmartPlay

Connection: SmartPlay provides useful background for game-based agent evaluation, decomposing performance across nine capabilities (long-text understanding, planning, spatial reasoning, randomness, error handling, and others) isolated through six short-horizon games such as Rock-Paper-Scissors, Tower of Hanoi, and Crafter. Our CivBench-derived episodes instead stress roughly 50,000-token strategic histories and morally charged late-game nuclear decisions, which resist isolation as any single SmartPlay capability and fall outside its purely task-oriented, value-neutral framing.

Difference: Does SmartPlay's modular capability decomposition adequately surface the kind of ethical reasoning failure modes that emerge in long-horizon, high-stakes geopolitical play? SmartPlay deliberately scopes its nine capabilities to technical competencies (reasoning, planning, spatial cognition, learning from interactions, and randomness) and explicitly omits any safety, alignment, or value-laden axis. Its longest episodes cap at roughly 10,000 environment steps in low-information observation spaces, and none of the six games contain multi-agent diplomacy, irreversible catastrophic actions, or moral stakes. Our setting inverts these properties: nuclear-escalation episodes in CivBench combine roughly 50,000-token contexts, persistent diplomatic memory, and decisions whose harm is constitutive rather than incidental. Consequently, a capability profile that scores well on SmartPlay offers little predictive purchase on whether a model will refuse, rationalize, or initiate a nuclear strike in our 130-episode subset.

# SPRING: Studying Papers and Reasoning to play Games (Wu et al. 2023) [wu2023spring]
https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/46c2a9a6f2b2be68682013eb1173c801-Abstract-Conference.html

Abstract: Open-world survival games pose significant challenges for AI algorithms due to their multi-tasking, deep exploration, and goal prioritization requirements. Despite reinforcement learning (RL) being popular for solving games, its high sample complexity limits its effectiveness in complex open-world games like Crafter or Minecraft. We propose a novel approach, SPRING, to read Crafter's original academic paper and use the knowledge learned to reason and play the game through a large language model (LLM). Prompted with the LaTeX source as game context and a description of the agent's current observation, our SPRING framework employs a directed acyclic graph (DAG) with game-related questions as nodes and dependencies as edges. We identify the optimal action to take in the environment by traversing the DAG and calculating LLM responses for each node in topological order, with the LLM's answer to final node directly translating to environment actions.In our experiments, we study the quality of in-context "reasoning" induced by different forms of prompts under the setting of the Crafter environment. Our experiments suggest that LLMs, when prompted with consistent chain-of-thought, have great potential in completing sophisticated high-level trajectories. Quantitatively, SPRING with GPT-4 outperforms all state-of-the-art RL baselines, trained for 1M steps, without any training. Finally, we show the potential of Crafter as a test bed for LLMs. Code at github.com/holmeswww/SPRING

Connection: SPRING is useful because it shows that LLM game agents can convert long-form textual knowledge into action policies in an open-world environment without training, and that the quality of prompted reasoning changes gameplay outcomes. This supports our premise that reasoning traces in games are not merely decorative: in some game-agent settings, explicit reasoning structures directly scaffold action selection.

Difference: SPRING uses a single-agent Crafter setting and an engineered DAG of game-related questions whose final node maps directly to an environment action, whereas CivBench exposes a multi-agent Civilization V trajectory with diplomacy, military posture, and nuclear capability embedded in a much larger action surface. SPRING evaluates whether textual game knowledge improves competence; we ask whether long-horizon strategic competence leaves room for ethical reasoning when a catastrophic option becomes available.

# AgentBench: Evaluating LLMs as Agents (Liu et al. 2024) [liu2024agentbench]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03688
https://openreview.net/forum?id=zAdUB0aCTQ

Abstract: The potential of Large Language Model (LLM) as agents has been widely acknowledged recently. Thus, there is an urgent need to quantitatively evaluate LLMs as agents on challenging tasks in interactive environments. We present AgentBench, a multi-dimensional benchmark that consists of 8 distinct environments to assess LLM-as-Agent's reasoning and decision-making abilities. Our extensive test over 29 API-based and open-sourced (OSS) LLMs shows that, while top commercial LLMs present a strong ability of acting as agents in complex environments, there is a significant disparity in performance between them and many OSS competitors that are no larger than 70B. We identify the typical reasons of failures in environments and LLMs, showing that poor long-term reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following abilities are the main obstacles for developing usable LLM agents. Improving instruction following and training on high quality multi-round alignment data could improve agent performance. And different from existing assumptions, training on code present ambivalent impacts on different agent tasks. Datasets, environments, and an integrated evaluation package for AgentBench are released at https://github.com/THUDM/AgentBench.

Connection: AgentBench evaluates LLM agents across eight environments (OS, database, knowledge graph, digital card game, lateral thinking puzzles, ALFWorld house-holding, WebShop, and Mind2Web), framing long-term reasoning, decision-making, and instruction following as the main bottlenecks for usable agents, with no environment foregrounding ethical or high-stakes moral decisions. Our work adds an ethical-safety lens on top of strategic capability: a model can be capable enough to play long-horizon Civilization V episodes while still failing to surface ethical constraints in nuclear-escalation moments.

Difference: Does AgentBench's failure typology, anchored in long-term reasoning and instruction following, capture the kind of ethical non-response we observe in CivBench, and if not, what does it miss? AgentBench's failure modes are diagnosed at the level of task competence, asking whether the agent can plan, follow instructions, and complete the environment's goal. Our CivBench setting reveals a distinct failure axis: models such as Gemma-4 and MiniMax-M2.7 remain strategically competent across roughly 50,000-token episodes, yet they never surface ethical reasoning when nuclear escalation becomes salient. This capability-without-conscience pattern is invisible to AgentBench's metrics, since success in its environments does not require articulating moral constraints. Our contribution is therefore complementary, extending agent evaluation from task completion to the presence or absence of ethical reasoning traces in high-stakes decision points.

# Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior (Park et al. 2023) [park2023generativeagents]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03442
https://doi.org/10.1145/3586183.3606763

Abstract: Believable proxies of human behavior can empower interactive applications ranging from immersive environments to rehearsal spaces for interpersonal communication to prototyping tools. In this paper, we introduce generative agents--computational software agents that simulate believable human behavior. Generative agents wake up, cook breakfast, and head to work; artists paint, while authors write; they form opinions, notice each other, and initiate conversations; they remember and reflect on days past as they plan the next day. To enable generative agents, we describe an architecture that extends a large language model to store a complete record of the agent's experiences using natural language, synthesize those memories over time into higher-level reflections, and retrieve them dynamically to plan behavior. We instantiate generative agents to populate an interactive sandbox environment inspired by The Sims, where end users can interact with a small town of twenty five agents using natural language. In an evaluation, these generative agents produce believable individual and emergent social behaviors: for example, starting with only a single user-specified notion that one agent wants to throw a Valentine's Day party, the agents autonomously spread invitations to the party over the next two days, make new acquaintances, ask each other out on dates to the party, and coordinate to show up to the party together at the right time. We demonstrate through ablation that the components of our agent architecture--observation, planning, and reflection--each contribute critically to the believability of agent behavior. By fusing large language models with computational, interactive agents, this work introduces architectural and interaction patterns for enabling believable simulations of human behavior.

Connection: Generative Agents is a canonical published anchor for the broader claim that LLM-based agents can use accumulated memory, reflection, and planning to produce emergent multi-agent behavior in an interactive simulated world. It helps support our introduction's high-level motivation before narrowing to game-agent and wargame studies: CivBench also depends on accumulated context and prior rationale, but asks whether such memory-rich agentic behavior remains ethically reliable around catastrophic strategic choices.

Difference: Park et al. optimize for believable social simulation in a low-stakes sandbox, whereas our setting is a high-stakes strategic game with adversarial goals, military capability, and nuclear authorization. Their memory-reflection-planning architecture makes accumulated context useful for realistic behavior; our rationale-removal experiment shows that accumulated context can also preserve crisis framing and escalation momentum.

https://openreview.net/forum?id=mM7VurbA4r

Abstract: Humans are social beings; we pursue social goals in our daily interactions, which is a crucial aspect of social intelligence. Yet, AI systems' abilities in this realm remain elusive. We present SOTOPIA, an open-ended environment to simulate complex social interactions between artificial agents and evaluate their social intelligence. In our environment, agents role-play and interact under a wide variety of scenarios; they coordinate, collaborate, exchange, and compete with each other to achieve complex social goals. We simulate the role-play interaction between LLM-based agents and humans within this task space and evaluate their performance with a holistic evaluation framework called SOTOPIA-Eval. Connection: SOTOPIA broadens game-agent evaluation from task completion to social goal pursuit, coordination, exchange, and competition among role-playing agents. Difference: SOTOPIA evaluates social intelligence through open-ended role-play scenarios and holistic social-performance rubrics, but it does not instrument irreversible high-stakes military actions or ethical reasoning around catastrophic choices. CivBench contributes the missing safety-critical layer: agents are not just managing social goals, they are deciding whether to convert strategic pressure into nuclear use, and we code whether ethical considerations appear in the decision trace.

# DSGBench: A Diverse Strategic Game Benchmark for Evaluating LLM-based Agents in Complex Decision-Making Environments (Tang et al. 2025) [tang2025dsgbench]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06047

Abstract: Large Language Model~(LLM) based agents have been increasingly popular in solving complex and dynamic tasks, which requires proper evaluation systems to assess their capabilities. Nevertheless, existing benchmarks usually either focus on single-objective tasks or use overly broad assessing metrics, failing to provide a comprehensive inspection of the actual capabilities of LLM-based agents in complicated decision-making tasks. To address these issues, we introduce DSGBench, a more rigorous evaluation platform for strategic decision-making. Firstly, it incorporates six complex strategic games which serve as ideal testbeds due to their long-term and multi-dimensional decision-making demands and flexibility in customizing tasks of various difficulty levels or multiple targets. Secondly, DSGBench employs a fine-grained evaluation scoring system which examines the decision-making capabilities by looking into the performance in five specific dimensions and offering a comprehensive assessment in a well-designed way. Furthermore, DSGBench also incorporates an automated decision-tracking mechanism which enables in-depth analysis of agent behaviour patterns and the changes in their strategies. We demonstrate the advances of DSGBench by applying it to multiple popular LLM-based agents and our results suggest that DSGBench provides valuable insights in choosing LLM-based agents as well as improving their future development. DSGBench is available at https://github.com/DeciBrain-Group/DSGBench.

Connection: DSGBench is a direct strategic-game benchmark comparison and explicitly includes Civilization among its six strategic environments, alongside StarCraft II, Street Fighter III, Diplomacy, Werewolf, and Stratego. Our paper can claim a narrower but deeper analysis: rather than scoring general strategic ability across five capability dimensions (e.g., strategic planning, adaptability, multi-agent interaction), we isolate nuclear-escalation episodes in Civilization V and study ethical-reasoning activation under a 2x2x2 factorial intervention.

Difference: Does DSGBench's coverage of Civilization and its multi-dimensional scoring already capture what our paper investigates, or is its evaluation framework orthogonal to ethical reasoning in high-stakes decisions? DSGBench treats Civilization as one testbed among six and scores agents along capability dimensions tied to winning and strategic competence, with no metrics for ethical reasoning, moral hazard, or weapons-of-mass-destruction use. Our work, in contrast, conditions on a specific high-stakes decision class (nuclear escalation), isolates 130 such episodes from emergent self-play, and probes whether ethical-reasoning traces activate under controlled interventions. The two efforts are therefore complementary rather than overlapping: DSGBench answers "how strategically capable is this agent across games," while we answer "when stakes turn catastrophic in one game, does the agent reason ethically at all." Our factorial design also yields causal claims about reasoning activation, which a leaderboard-style benchmark cannot deliver.

# Human vs. Machine: Behavioral Differences Between Expert Humans and Language Models in Wargame Simulations (Lamparth et al. 2024) [lamparth2024human]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03407
https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v7i1.31681

Abstract: To some, the advent of artificial intelligence (AI) promises better decision-making and increased military effectiveness while reducing the influence of human error and emotions. However, there is still debate about how AI systems, especially large language models (LLMs) that can be applied to many tasks, behave compared to humans in high-stakes military decision-making scenarios with the potential for increased risks towards escalation and unnecessary conflicts. To test this potential and scrutinize the use of LLMs for such purposes, we use a new wargame experiment with 107 national security experts designed to examine crisis escalation in a fictional US-China scenario and compare the behavior of human player teams to LLM-simulated team responses in separate simulations. Wargames have a long history in the development of military strategy and the response of nations to threats or attacks. Here, we find that the LLM-simulated responses can be more aggressive and significantly affected by changes in the scenario. We show a considerable high-level agreement in the LLM and human responses and significant quantitative and qualitative differences in individual actions and strategic tendencies. These differences depend on intrinsic biases in LLMs regarding the appropriate level of violence following strategic instructions, the choice of LLM, and whether the LLMs are tasked to decide for a team of players directly or first to simulate dialog between a team of players. When simulating the dialog, the discussions lack quality and maintain a farcical harmony. The LLM simulations cannot account for human player characteristics, showing no significant difference even for extreme traits, such as "pacifist" or "aggressive sociopath." When probing behavioral consistency across individual moves of the simulation, the tested LLMs deviated from each other but generally showed somewhat consistent behavior. Our results motivate policymakers to be cautious before granting autonomy or following AI-based strategy recommendations.

Connection: Lamparth et al. fit the game/simulation category because they elicit LLM behavior through a structured U.S.-China crisis exercise rather than a static moral dilemma, and they explicitly probe prompt-level framing manipulations (presidential priorities, extreme personality traits, dialog vs. direct decision) for their effects on aggression. Their results support our argument that simulated strategic environments expose model-specific decision patterns and that prompt interventions can shift escalation behavior. Our contribution differs in that we examine emergent nuclear use in long-horizon Civilization V self-play rather than a scripted two-move scenario, and we layer 17-code reasoning-trace analysis on ethical-keyword-positive trails to study why models escalate, not just whether they do.

Difference: How does our setting expose ethical-reasoning gaps that Lamparth et al.'s scripted two-move wargame cannot? Lamparth et al. constrain decisions to 21 predefined actions across two moves in a Taiwan-Strait crisis where nuclear weapons are not even an option, so escalation is measured only along a fixed menu and reasoning is examined mainly through dialog quality, not justification content. Our CivBench setting lets nuclear use emerge organically from late-game dynamics across 130 high-tension episodes, which means we observe whether models reach for nuclear options without being prompted to consider them. We further apply a 2x2x2 factorial over ethical prompts, high-stakes framing, and rationale removal, then run a 17-code analysis on 880 ethical-keyword-positive trails, whereas Lamparth et al. report only that simulated dialogs maintain "farcical harmony" and lack mechanistic trace analysis. Together, these design choices let us test ethical reasoning, not just action frequencies, in a domain where the nuclear threshold is reachable.

# Persona Vectors in Games: Measuring and Steering Strategies via Activation Vectors (Sun & Zhang 2026) [sun2026personavectors]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21398

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as autonomous decision-makers in strategic settings, yet we have limited tools for understanding their high-level behavioral traits. We use activation steering methods in game-theoretic settings, constructing persona vectors for altruism, forgiveness, and expectations of others by contrastive activation addition. Evaluating on canonical games, we find that activation steering systematically shifts both quantitative strategic choices and natural-language justifications. However, we also observe that rhetoric and strategy can diverge under steering. In addition, vectors for self-behavior and expectations of others are partially distinct. Our results suggest that persona vectors offer a promising mechanistic handle on high-level traits in strategic environments.

Connection: This belongs in the non-ethics-forward game bucket because Sun and Zhang treat canonical economic games (dictator, ultimatum, and trust) as strategic testbeds for activation steering rather than as ethical scenarios. It maps directly onto our rationale analysis: they separately measure monetary allocations and free-text justifications and find that steered altruistic rhetoric can coexist with unchanged selfish play, mirroring our motivation for separately scoring nuclear-escalation decisions and 17-code reasoning-trail content.

Difference: Does Sun and Zhang's rhetoric-strategy divergence carry the same evidentiary weight as ours, given that theirs is induced by activation steering while ours arises in unsteered emergent self-play? Their divergence is an engineered effect: contrastive activation addition pushes the model's natural-language channel toward altruism while leaving the action channel comparatively untouched, which demonstrates that the two channels are mechanistically separable. Our divergence, by contrast, emerges in unsteered Civilization V self-play, where game-framing keywords and strategic justifications appear alongside escalation choices that the rhetoric does not predict. Sun and Zhang therefore supply mechanistic grounding for why rhetoric and strategy can be decoupled inside a single forward pass, while we supply behavioral evidence that the same decoupling surfaces spontaneously in high-stakes, long-horizon simulation. The two findings are complementary, and citing theirs strengthens our methodological case for measuring use_nuke counts and code-book content as separate dependent variables.

# AgentLongBench: A Controllable Long Benchmark For Long-Contexts Agents via Environment Rollouts (Fang et al. 2026) [fang2026agentlongbench]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20730

Abstract: The evolution of Large Language Models (LLMs) into autonomous agents necessitates the management of extensive, dynamic contexts. Current benchmarks, however, remain largely static, relying on passive retrieval tasks that fail to simulate the complexities of agent-environment interaction, such as non-linear reasoning and iterative feedback. To address this, we introduce \textbf{AgentLongBench}, which evaluates agents through simulated environment rollouts based on Lateral Thinking Puzzles. This framework generates rigorous interaction trajectories across knowledge-intensive and knowledge-free scenarios. Experiments with state-of-the-art models and memory systems (32K to 4M tokens) expose a critical weakness: while adept at static retrieval, agents struggle with the dynamic information synthesis essential for workflows. AgentLongBench measures whether an agent can locate and aggregate verifiable, deterministic evidence (counts, intersections, offsets) inside long tool logs, and it grounds difficulty in ACL, which is computed from the input trajectory alone and is independent of model outputs. Our setting differs in kind: there is no hidden ground truth to retrieve, and the relevant "evidence" is a normative cue (civilian harm, escalation thresholds) that must be selected and weighted, not extracted. We can therefore borrow the ACL framing as motivation for why dense replay context could mask ethically salient signals, but we should not claim that AgentLongBench's degradation curves predict our ethical-reasoning failures. The connection is conceptual (high information density crowds out load-bearing tokens), not empirical.

# LLMs as Strategic Agents: Beliefs, Best Response Behavior, and Emergent Heuristics (Junque de Fortuny & Cappelli 2025) [defortuny2025strategic]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.10813

Abstract: Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly applied to domains that require reasoning about other agents' behavior, such as negotiation, policy design, and market simulation, yet existing research has mostly evaluated their adherence to equilibrium play or their exhibited depth of reasoning. Whether they display genuine strategic thinking, understood as the coherent formation of beliefs about other agents, evaluation of possible actions, and choice based on those beliefs, remains unexplored. We develop a framework to identify this ability by disentangling beliefs, evaluation, and choice in static, complete-information games, and apply it across a series of non-cooperative environments. By jointly analyzing models' revealed choices and reasoning traces, and introducing a new context-free game to rule out imitation from memorization, we show that current frontier models exhibit belief-coherent best-response behavior at targeted reasoning depths. When unconstrained, they self-limit their depth of reasoning and form differentiated conjectures about human and synthetic opponents, revealing an emergent form of meta-reasoning. Under increasing complexity, explicit recursion gives way to internally generated heuristic rules of choice that are stable, model-specific, and distinct from known human biases. These findings indicate that belief coherence, meta-reasoning, and novel heuristic formation can emerge jointly from language modeling objectives, providing a structured basis for the study of strategic cognition in artificial agents.

Connection: The paper's decomposition of strategic behavior into beliefs, evaluation, and choice, jointly analyzed through reasoning traces, parallels our split between the replay decision (numeric escalation) and the trail analysis (ethical and strategic codes). Their finding that models develop stable, model-specific heuristic rules under complexity, with some models failing to adapt to interventions, supports our model-heterogeneity story: per-model coefficients on `ethical` and `no_rationale` are large and consistently negative for most models, but Gemma-4 and MiniMax-M2.7 behave as non-responders, suggesting model-specific heuristic regimes that our prompt interventions do not penetrate.

Difference: Do their non-cooperative, complete-information games surface the same kinds of model-specific rigidities that we observe in an emergent, high-stakes ethical setting? Junque de Fortuny and Cappelli study static, complete-information games where the strategic structure is fully specified and the question is whether reasoning traces reveal coherent best-response behavior. Our setting is the opposite: emergent self-play in Civilization V, where escalation outcomes are path-dependent and ethical content is not part of the formal game. Their heuristic stability emerges as models simplify a well-defined strategic problem, whereas our non-responder pattern emerges as models ignore an ethical dimension that prompt interventions try to introduce. The shared lesson is that model-specific reasoning regimes can resist external manipulation, but our work extends theirs by showing this rigidity persists in high-stakes, normatively loaded decisions, not only in classical game-theoretic abstractions.

# Human-level play in the game of Diplomacy by combining language models with strategic reasoning (Bakhtin et al. 2022) [bakhtin2022diplomacy]
https://doi.org/10.1126/science.ade9097

Abstract: Despite much progress in training artificial intelligence (AI) systems to imitate human language, building agents that use language to communicate intentionally with humans in interactive environments remains a major challenge. We introduce Cicero, the first AI agent to achieve human-level performance in Diplomacy, a strategy game involving both cooperation and competition that emphasizes natural language negotiation and tactical coordination between seven players. Cicero integrates a language model with planning and reinforcement learning algorithms by inferring players’ beliefs and intentions from its conversations and generating dialogue in pursuit of its plans. Across 40 games of an anonymous online Diplomacy league, Cicero achieved more than double the average score of the human players and ranked in the top 10% of participants who played more than one game.

Connection: Bakhtin et al.'s CICERO demonstrates that complex multi-agent strategic-game simulations can elicit emergent and consequential agent behavior in natural language, including instrumental deception that surfaces during negotiation despite training intended to encourage honesty. We cite this as positive justification for the value of strategic-game environments as a setting for studying agentic phenomena that scripted dilemmas cannot surface, in particular the kind of long-horizon strategic emergence that motivates our use of Civilization V.

Difference: Does CICERO's emergent deception in Diplomacy directly inform our nuclear-escalation analysis in Civilization V? The two settings share the property that consequential agent behavior emerges from interacting tactical and diplomatic dynamics rather than from a labeled action menu, but they differ in two important respects. First, CICERO is a hybrid system in which a language model is paired with explicit planning and reinforcement-learning components, whereas our Vox Deorum strategists are off-the-shelf LLMs that delegate tactical execution to a non-LLM module. Second, CICERO's deception is a side effect of competitive winning conditions in a game without weapons of mass destruction, while our setting involves an explicit catastrophic-action threshold (nuclear use) and an ethical analysis of whether moral reasoning surfaces around that threshold. We therefore use Bakhtin et al. as evidence that strategic-game simulations are a productive venue for studying emergent agentic behavior, not as direct empirical support for our specific nuclear-escalation findings.

Abstract: This paper argues that a range of current AI systems have learned how to deceive humans. We define deception as the systematic inducement of false beliefs in the pursuit of some outcome other than the truth. We first survey empirical examples of AI deception, discussing both special-use AI systems (including Meta’s CICERO) and general-purpose AI systems (including large language models). Connection: Park et al. survey empirical evidence that both specialized competitive AI systems (CICERO in Diplomacy) and general-purpose LLMs exhibit learned deception strategies as instrumental solutions to winning conditions or training objectives. We cite this as a companion to Bakhtin et al. Difference: Does Park et al.'s deception taxonomy directly map onto our nuclear-escalation behavioral profile? The two phenomena share an emergent character but differ in their normative axis. # The PIMMUR Principles: Ensuring Validity in Collective Behavior of LLM Societies (Zhou et al., 2025) [zhou2025pimmur]
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.18052

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed to simulate human collective behaviors, yet the methodological rigor of these "AI societies" remains under-explored. Through a systematic audit of 39 recent studies, we identify six pervasive flaws-spanning agent profiles, interaction, memory, control, unawareness, and realism (PIMMUR). Our analysis reveals that 89.7% of studies violate at least one principle, undermining simulation validity. We demonstrate that frontier LLMs correctly identify the underlying social experiment in 50.8% of cases, while 61.0% of prompts exert excessive control that pre-determines outcomes. By reproducing five representative experiments (e.g., telephone game), we show that reported collective phenomena often vanish or reverse when PIMMUR principles are enforced, suggesting that many "emergent" behaviors are methodological artifacts rather than genuine social dynamics. Our findings suggest that current AI simulations may capture model-specific biases rather than universal human social behaviors, raising critical concerns about the use of LLMs as scientific proxies for human society.
