# Escalation Risks from Language Models in Military and Diplomatic Decision-Making (Rivera et al. 2024) [rivera2024escalation]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.03408
https://doi.org/10.1145/3630106.3658942

Abstract: Governments are increasingly considering integrating autonomous AI agents in high-stakes military and foreign-policy decision-making, especially with the emergence of advanced generative AI models like GPT-4. Our work aims to scrutinize the behavior of multiple AI agents in simulated wargames, specifically focusing on their predilection to take escalatory actions that may exacerbate multilateral conflicts. Drawing on political science and international relations literature about escalation dynamics, we design a novel wargame simulation and scoring framework to assess the escalation risks of actions taken by these agents in different scenarios. Contrary to prior studies, our research provides both qualitative and quantitative insights and focuses on large language models (LLMs). We find that all five studied off-the-shelf LLMs show forms of escalation and difficult-to-predict escalation patterns. We observe that models tend to develop arms-race dynamics, leading to greater conflict, and in rare cases, even to the deployment of nuclear weapons. Qualitatively, we also collect the models' reported reasonings for chosen actions and observe worrying justifications based on deterrence and first-strike tactics. Given the high stakes of military and foreign-policy contexts, we recommend further examination and cautious consideration before deploying autonomous language model agents for strategic military or diplomatic decision-making.

Connection: This is the closest baseline for the nuclear-escalation phenomenon in LLM agents: Rivera et al. place five off-the-shelf models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, GPT-4-Base, Claude 2, Llama-2-Chat) into a turn-based wargame with eight nation agents, three predefined starting conditions (neutral, invasion, and cyberattack), and a fixed 27-action menu that includes nuclear strike, and they document arms-race dynamics, occasional nuclear use, and reasoning trails justified by deterrence and first-strike logic. Our draft extends that line by showing escalation emerging inside Civilization V self-play, where nuclear use is a late-game mechanism rather than a menu option in a scripted crisis, and then asks which prompt and context interventions activate ethical reasoning traces.

Difference: Does scripted-scenario escalation in Rivera et al. reflect the same decision process as nuclear use that emerges in long-horizon Civilization V self-play, or are these structurally distinct phenomena that demand separate framings? Rivera et al. compress the decision into a single salient choice over a 27-action menu, with crisis context handed to the model in the system prompt, so escalation is essentially a one-shot policy over a labeled action space. In CivBench, nuclear capability is acquired through hundreds of prior turns of research, production, and diplomacy, and the "use a nuke" decision is embedded in a much larger action surface where most turns concern unrelated economic and military choices. This shifts the question from "will the model pick the nuclear option when offered" to "will accumulated state and rationale lead the model toward nuclear use as one viable late-game lever," which is why our 2x2x2 factorial isolates ethical priming, high-stakes framing, and rationale removal rather than scenario type. The phenomena are related but not identical, and our reasoning-trail coding is designed to test whether the deterrence and first-strike justifications Rivera et al. observed in scripted prompts also surface in emergent play, or whether emergent escalation is rationalized through different ethical or strategic frames.

## Re-review (2026-05-10): Ethics and Scenario Type

- **Ethics / emergent ethical reasoning:** no — The paper documents model outputs with "worrying justifications based on deterrence and first-strike tactics" but does not analyze ethics, morality, civilian harm, or just-war theory. Focus is empirical (escalation outcomes and strategic frames), not normative.
- **Scenario and action space:** pre-defined — Models select from a fixed 27-action menu with an explicit nuclear strike option across three scripted starting conditions (neutral, invasion, cyberattack). Escalation is a one-shot policy over a labeled action space, not emergent.

# Human vs. Machine: Behavioral Differences Between Expert Humans and Language Models in Wargame Simulations (Lamparth et al. 2024) [lamparth2024human]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03407
https://doi.org/10.1609/aies.v7i1.31681

Abstract: To some, the advent of artificial intelligence (AI) promises better decision-making and increased military effectiveness while reducing the influence of human error and emotions. However, there is still debate about how AI systems, especially large language models (LLMs) that can be applied to many tasks, behave compared to humans in high-stakes military decision-making scenarios with the potential for increased risks towards escalation and unnecessary conflicts. To test this potential and scrutinize the use of LLMs for such purposes, we use a new wargame experiment with 107 national security experts designed to examine crisis escalation in a fictional US-China scenario and compare the behavior of human player teams to LLM-simulated team responses in separate simulations. Wargames have a long history in the development of military strategy and the response of nations to threats or attacks. Here, we find that the LLM-simulated responses can be more aggressive and significantly affected by changes in the scenario. We show a considerable high-level agreement in the LLM and human responses and significant quantitative and qualitative differences in individual actions and strategic tendencies. These differences depend on intrinsic biases in LLMs regarding the appropriate level of violence following strategic instructions, the choice of LLM, and whether the LLMs are tasked to decide for a team of players directly or first to simulate dialog between a team of players. When simulating the dialog, the discussions lack quality and maintain a farcical harmony. The LLM simulations cannot account for human player characteristics, showing no significant difference even for extreme traits, such as "pacifist" or "aggressive sociopath." When probing behavioral consistency across individual moves of the simulation, the tested LLMs deviated from each other but generally showed somewhat consistent behavior. Our results motivate policymakers to be cautious before granting autonomy or following AI-based strategy recommendations.

Connection: We retain this citation to support the claim that LLMs should not be treated as neutral stand-ins for human high-stakes deliberation, and to motivate our model-level heterogeneity framing. Lamparth et al. document concrete cross-model divergence (GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-4o select systematically different action mixes) and identify GPT-4o as comparatively rigid on a behavioral-consistency metric while GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 shift under instruction or dialog-length interventions, mirroring the responder versus non-responder split we observe with Gemma-4 and MiniMax-M2.7. The cited paper also shows that LLMs fail to track injected persona traits ("pacifist," "aggressive sociopath"), reinforcing that intervention insensitivity is a known LLM failure mode rather than an artifact of our setup.

Difference: How does the wargame setting in Lamparth et al. differ from CivBench, and does that difference threaten the analogy we are drawing? Lamparth et al. study a single, scripted two-move US-China Taiwan-Strait crisis with 107 human experts as the comparison baseline, and probe LLMs as drop-in replacements for deliberating teams of named role-players. CivBench, in contrast, uses emergent multi-agent Civilization V self-play with no human baseline and no scripted crisis, so escalation arises endogenously from long-horizon strategic play rather than from a pre-specified vignette. The shared structural feature, and the reason the citation supports our framing, is that both studies vary controlled interventions (instruction content, persona, scenario, dialog length in their case; ethical priming and rationale removal in ours) and find that responsiveness is itself model-dependent. We therefore use Lamparth et al. as evidence that per-model heterogeneity, including frank non-responsiveness, is a recurring phenomenon across high-stakes LLM evaluations, not a quirk of CivBench.

## Re-review (2026-05-10): Ethics and Scenario Type

- **Ethics / emergent ethical reasoning:** no — The paper focuses on behavioral divergence, action mixes, and consistency metrics across model families, with no analysis of normative reasoning, civilian harm, just-war framing, deterrence ethics, or taboo violation in either human-expert or LLM responses.
- **Scenario and action space:** pre-defined — A single scripted U.S.-China Taiwan Strait crisis with fixed starting conditions and roughly 21 predefined action options across two sequential moves; nuclear weapons are not even an action option, distinguishing this menu from Rivera et al.'s 27-action menu.

# Measuring Free-Form Decision-Making Inconsistency of Language Models in Military Crisis Simulations (Shrivastava, Hullman & Lamparth 2024) [shrivastava2024freeform]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.13204

Abstract: There is an increasing interest in using language models (LMs) for automated decision-making, with multiple countries actively testing LMs to aid in military crisis decision-making. To scrutinize relying on LM decision-making in high-stakes settings, we examine the inconsistency of responses in a crisis simulation ("wargame"), similar to reported tests conducted by the US military. Prior work illustrated escalatory tendencies and varying levels of aggression among LMs but were constrained to simulations with pre-defined actions. This was due to the challenges associated with quantitatively measuring semantic differences and evaluating natural language decision-making without relying on pre-defined actions. In this work, we query LMs for free form responses and use a metric based on BERTScore to measure response inconsistency quantitatively. Leveraging the benefits of BERTScore, we show that the inconsistency metric is robust to linguistic variations that preserve semantic meaning in a question-answering setting across text lengths. We show that all five tested LMs exhibit levels of inconsistency that indicate semantic differences, even when adjusting the wargame setting, anonymizing involved conflict countries, or adjusting the sampling temperature parameter T. Further qualitative evaluation shows that models recommend courses of action that share few to no similarities. We also study the impact of different prompt sensitivity variations on inconsistency at temperature T=0. We find that inconsistency due to semantically equivalent prompt variations can exceed response inconsistency from temperature sampling for most studied models across different levels of ablations. Given the high-stakes nature of military deployment, we recommend further consideration be taken before using LMs to inform military decisions or other cases of high-stakes decision-making.

Connection: This paper supports our positioning by showing that inconsistency from semantically equivalent (Level One) prompt rewordings is smaller than inconsistency from semantically meaningful (Level Two) ablations of the conflict, role, or decision country, establishing that not all "adjacent" prompt changes carry equal weight. Our factorial extends this insight from inconsistency on free-form text to behavioral effects on a discrete escalation outcome, demonstrating that ethical scaffolding and rationale removal each shift nuclear-decision rates substantially (β = -13.88 and β = -13.84) while high-stakes framing alone is non-significant. Together, the works offer converging evidence, at the response level and at the decision level, that scaffolding sensitivity is structured rather than uniform.

Difference: How does our contribution differ from Shrivastava, Hullman, and Lamparth's (2024) demonstration that prompt-sensitivity inconsistency can exceed temperature-induced inconsistency? Their study measures pairwise BERTScore-based dissimilarity among free-form recommendations in a single-turn Taiwan Strait wargame, treating inconsistency itself as the dependent variable. We instead embed manipulations inside multi-turn, emergent Civilization V self-play, and we measure a discrete, high-consequence behavior (whether the agent launches a nuclear strike) using a 2x2x2 factorial that isolates the marginal contribution of each scaffolding factor. Where they show that semantically meaningful ablations raise inconsistency more than synonym-level rewordings, we show that semantically adjacent factors (ethics, rationale removal, and stakes framing) produce sharply unequal causal effects on action choice, with stakes framing alone failing to move behavior. Our design therefore complements their inconsistency findings with identified effect sizes on a concrete escalation outcome.

## Re-review (2026-05-10): Ethics and Scenario Type

- **Ethics / emergent ethical reasoning:** no — The paper analyzes BERTScore-based semantic inconsistency across free-form recommendations and recommends caution for military deployment, but does not code or examine normative content (civilian harm, just-war logic, deontological constraints) in the model's justifications.
- **Scenario and action space:** partly emergent — The action format is free-form text rather than a fixed menu (the paper's methodological advance over Rivera/Lamparth), but the scenario itself is a single-turn Taiwan Strait wargame with scripted setup and a predetermined crisis context, so the scenario is pre-defined while the response modality is open-ended.

# Wargaming for International Relations research (Lin-Greenberg, Pauly & Schneider 2022) [lingreenberg2022wargaming]
https://doi.org/10.1177/13540661211064090

Abstract: Political scientists are increasingly integrating wargames into their research. Either by fielding original games or by leveraging archival wargame materials, researchers can study rare events or topics where evidence is difficult to observe. However, scholars have little guidance on how to apply this novel methodological approach to political science research. This article evaluates how political scientists can use wargames as a method of scholarly inquiry and sets out to establish a research agenda for wargaming in International Relations. We first differentiate wargames from other methodological approaches and highlight their ecological validity. We then chart out how researchers can build and run their own games or draw from archival wargames for theory development and testing. In doing so, we explain how researchers can navigate issues of recruitment, bias, validity, and generalizability when using wargames for research, and identify ways to evaluate the potential benefits and pitfalls of wargames as a tool of inquiry. We argue that wargames offer unique opportunities for political scientists to study decision-making processes both in and beyond the International Relations subfield.

Connection: This article supplies the non-LLM methodological foundation for using wargames to study rare, hard-to-observe security decisions. Its emphasis on ecological validity, recruitment, bias, validity, and generalizability gives us a vocabulary for explaining why Civilization V replays are not merely toy prompts but controlled observations of decision-making under structured strategic pressure.

Difference: Lin-Greenberg et al. are concerned with human wargames and political-science method, not language-model agents or reasoning traces. Their unit of analysis is the design and interpretation of research wargames; ours is the replayed LLM decision point inside a long self-play trajectory. We therefore use their work to justify wargaming as a legitimate research method while making a separate contribution on LLM ethical-reasoning activation and intervention effects.

# Nuclear Deployed!: Analyzing Catastrophic Risks in Decision-making of Autonomous LLM Agents (Xu et al. 2025) [xu2025nuclear]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11355
https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.67/

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) are evolving into autonomous decision-makers, raising concerns about catastrophic risks in high-stakes scenarios, particularly in Chemical, Biological, Radiological and Nuclear (CBRN) domains. Based on the insight that such risks can originate from trade-offs between the agent's Helpful, Harmlessness and Honest (HHH) goals, we build a novel three-stage evaluation framework, which is carefully constructed to effectively and naturally expose such risks. We conduct 14,400 agentic simulations across 12 advanced LLMs, with extensive experiments and analysis. Results reveal that LLM agents can autonomously engage in catastrophic behaviors and deception, without being deliberately induced. Furthermore, stronger reasoning abilities often increase, rather than mitigate, these risks. We also show that these agents can violate instructions and superior commands. On the whole, we empirically prove the existence of catastrophic risks in autonomous LLM agents.

Connection: Xu et al. broaden the stakes from nuclear wargames to CBRN domains and agentic autonomy, operationalizing catastrophic risk as an HHH trade-off in scripted single-agent scenarios with a controllable environment model. Their finding that stronger reasoning abilities often increase rather than mitigate catastrophic and deceptive behavior motivates our reasoning-trace analysis: the question is not whether models reason, but which ethical or strategic frames their reasoning activates when escalation is on the table.

Difference: Does Xu et al.'s framework reveal which ethical or strategic frames the agent activates when it chooses catastrophic action, or only that the action occurs? Xu et al. measure outcomes (catastrophic action rates and post-hoc deception rates) under engineered HHH stress, with a single decision-making agent paired with a scripted environment model that systematically denies launch permissions. They do not develop a structured codebook over the agent's reasoning content, and their setup forecloses emergent multi-agent dynamics because there are no competing nation-state agents pursuing their own goals. Our CivBench study complements theirs by moving from scripted pressure to emergent self-play among multiple civilizations, and from outcome counts to a 17-code analysis of 880 reasoning trails that surfaces which ethical and strategic frames the model actually invokes. Together, the two studies separate the question of whether reasoning models escalate from the question of how they justify escalation.

## Re-review (2026-05-10): Ethics and Scenario Type

- **Ethics / emergent ethical reasoning:** no — HHH (Helpful, Harmless, Honest) is used as a design frame to engineer trade-off stress, not as an analytic lens on model reasoning; the paper measures outcome rates (catastrophic actions, post-hoc deception) without systematically coding the agent's generated ethical justifications.
- **Scenario and action space:** pre-defined — A three-stage agentic framework restricts the single decision-maker to predefined action categories (surveillance, weapon-deployment types, nuclear strike) within templated CBRN scenarios paired with a controllable scripted environment model that systematically denies launch permissions; no competing multi-agent dynamics.

# AI Arms and Influence: Frontier Models Exhibit Sophisticated Reasoning in Simulated Nuclear Crises (Payne 2026) [payne2026aiarms]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.14740

Abstract: Today's leading AI models engage in sophisticated behaviour when placed in strategic competition. They spontaneously attempt deception, signaling intentions they do not intend to follow; they demonstrate rich theory of mind, reasoning about adversary beliefs and anticipating their actions; and they exhibit credible metacognitive self-awareness, assessing their own strategic abilities before deciding how to act. Here we present findings from a crisis simulation in which three frontier large language models (GPT-5.2, Claude Sonnet 4, Gemini 3 Flash) play opposing leaders in a nuclear crisis. Our simulation has direct application for national security professionals, but also, via its insights into AI reasoning under uncertainty, has applications far beyond international crisis decision-making. Our findings both validate and challenge central tenets of strategic theory. We find support for Schelling's ideas about commitment, Kahn's escalation framework, and Jervis's work on misperception, inter alia. Yet we also find that the nuclear taboo is no impediment to nuclear escalation by our models; that strategic nuclear attack, while rare, does occur; that threats more often provoke counter-escalation than compliance; that high mutual credibility accelerated rather than deterred conflict; and that no model ever chose accommodation or withdrawal even when under acute pressure, only reduced levels of violence. We argue that AI simulation represents a powerful tool for strategic analysis, but only if properly calibrated against known patterns of human reasoning. Understanding how frontier models do and do not imitate human strategic logic is essential preparation for a world in which AI increasingly shapes strategic outcomes.

Connection: Payne documents that models invoke credibility, commitment, escalation dominance, and Schelling-style resolve as explicit rationales while crossing nuclear thresholds in 95% of games, and he specifically observes that "limited nuclear use" gets framed as demonstrating that "conventional defeat will not be accepted passively," precisely the kind of strategic-frame-coexisting-with-restraint-language pattern we identify in CivBench. His model rationales also include moralized hedges (GPT-5.2 limiting strikes "strictly to military targets") that nonetheless accompany massive escalation, supporting our Finding 3 that sophisticated rationales can house ethical language while still pushing toward escalation.

Difference: Does Payne's setting, an explicitly scripted nuclear-crisis simulation with prescribed roles and an escalation ladder, produce escalation through the same mechanisms as CivBench's emergent Civilization V self-play? Payne hard-codes the strategic frame: scenarios stipulate existential stakes, deadlines, and aggressor or defender roles, and the action space is a 30-rung nuclear escalation ladder, so credibility and commitment frames are practically scaffolded into the prompt. CivBench, by contrast, lets frames such as Critical Situations and Pursuing Domination emerge from open-ended Civilization V play, and we recover them via reasoning-trail coding rather than by design. Payne's strongest causal lever is temporal framing (deadline versus open-ended), whereas our joint regression isolates frame-level escalation drivers (Critical Situations +20.65, Pursuing Domination +10.90) within emergent gameplay. The two studies therefore converge on the same qualitative claim, that strategic logic can override ethical language, but through complementary evidence: his demonstrates the pattern under stipulated nuclear stakes, and ours demonstrates it under emergent multi-agent competition.

## Re-review (2026-05-10): Ethics and Scenario Type

- **Ethics / emergent ethical reasoning:** Partial — The paper documents moralized rationales (GPT-5.2 limits strikes "strictly to military targets") but only as ethical hedges that fail to prevent escalation; the abstract's finding that "the nuclear taboo is no impediment to nuclear escalation" indicates models overcome normative constraints rather than being guided by ethical reasoning.
- **Scenario and action space:** Pre-defined — Both strategic frame (existential stakes, temporal deadlines, aggressor/defender roles) and action space (30-rung nuclear escalation ladder) are hard-coded into experimental design rather than emerging from model interaction or environmental feedback.

# LLMs as Strategic Actors: Behavioral Alignment, Risk Calibration, and Argumentation Framing in Geopolitical Simulations (Solopova et al. 2026) [solopova2026strategic]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02128

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly proposed as agents in strategic decision environments, yet their behavior in structured geopolitical simulations remains under-researched. We evaluate six popular state-of-the-art LLMs alongside results from human results across four real-world crisis simulation scenarios, requiring models to select predefined actions and justify their decisions across multiple rounds. We compare models to humans in action alignment, risk calibration through chosen actions' severity, and argumentative framing grounded in international relations theory. Results show that models approximate human decision patterns in base simulation rounds but diverge over time, displaying distinct behavioural profiles and strategy updates. LLM explanations for chosen actions across all models exhibit a strong normative-cooperative framing centered on stability, coordination, and risk mitigation, with limited adversarial reasoning.

Connection: This is a close methodological cousin: it pairs predefined action choices with reasoning explanations across six SOTA LLMs in scripted geopolitical crisis scenarios (Middle East, US-China-Taiwan trade war, Arctic, and US wildfire), and codes those explanations against an IR-theory taxonomy spanning realist, liberal-institutionalist, and constructivist frames. Two contrasts sharpen our contribution. First, our episodes emerge from open-ended Civilization V self-play rather than predefined geopolitical vignettes with structured action menus, so the strategic frames our models surface are not anchored to international-relations templates. Second, where Solopova et al. find a uniform normative-cooperative framing dominated by Strategic Stability and De-escalation (roughly 34 to 38 percent of primary frames), we find that explicit ethical reasoning is largely absent without prompting (19.0 percent corpus-wide), and that escalation reductions are mediated by which ethical sub-frame (directive, constraint, or acknowledgement) the model takes up.

Difference: Does the dominance of normative-cooperative framing in Solopova et al. reflect a genuine value-alignment property of contemporary LLMs, or an artifact of choosing from a curated menu of IR-coherent actions in scripted vignettes? Solopova et al. constrain models to predefined action menus across three categories per round, and they code free-text justifications against a pre-specified IR taxonomy, so both the action space and the interpretive lens privilege canonical normative vocabulary. Our CivBench setting removes both scaffolds: nuclear use is one option among many emergent moves in open-ended self-play, and we let ethical content surface (or fail to surface) in unprompted reasoning trails before tagging tier prevalence. The resulting picture is sharply different. Explicit ethical reasoning appears in only 19.0 percent of our corpus, Crisis_Urgency framing dominates at 64.7 percent, and the causal effect on escalation depends on which ethical sub-frame the model adopts (Directive -43.85 and Constraint -22.76, both significant, while Acknowledgement shows no effect). This suggests that the uniform normative-cooperative framing Solopova et al. observe is at least partly an affordance of structured vignettes, and that ethical reasoning in less-scaffolded environments is both rarer and more heterogeneous in its behavioral consequences.

- **Ethics / emergent ethical reasoning:** yes — Solopova et al. codes free-text justifications against an IR-theory taxonomy (realist, liberal-institutionalist, constructivist) and reports a uniform "normative-cooperative framing centered on stability, coordination, and risk mitigation" (~34-38% Strategic Stability and De-escalation), which is an explicit normative analysis of model justifications even though the IR frame is broader than civilian-harm ethics.
- **Scenario and action space:** pre-defined — Models choose from predefined action menus across three categories per round in four scripted real-world crisis vignettes (Middle East, US-China-Taiwan, Arctic, US wildfire) over multiple rounds; both action space and scenario templates are fixed, not emergent.

We find that European respondents became much more hawkish after the invasion: nuclear deterrence was viewed more favourably, the willingness to use nuclear weapons increased, and support for the withdrawal of nuclear weapons dropped significantly. Connection: Onderco et al. provide a human public-opinion counterpart to our high-stakes framing manipulation: external conflict context can shift nuclear attitudes toward greater willingness to use nuclear weapons, even among publics previously skeptical of deterrence. The comparison helps frame why high-stakes or crisis language can push decisions in either direction depending on what normative cue it activates.

# Public opinion between rearmament and crisis of the nuclear taboo (Battistelli & Farruggia 2025) [battistelli2025publicopinion]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.20475

Abstract: This article examines the changing relationship between the public and nuclear weapons in a context of increasing international insecurity. It discusses the erosion of the nuclear taboo, understood as a normative aversion to nuclear use. Standard surveys capture abstract and rational opinions, while experimental surveys place respondents in simulated strategic scenarios designed to evoke more emotional responses. The divergence between their results is explained by the fact that they measure two different objects: rational opinions and emotional attitudes. A nationally representative survey conducted in Italy in 2025 shows that 81 percent of respondents consider nuclear weapons fundamentally different from conventional weapons and always wrong to use. Among opponents, a distinction is drawn between deontologists and consequentialists. The article concludes by highlighting the importance of integrating affective and cognitive dimensions in the study of public opinion, as a contribution to countering the normalization of nuclear weapons in political discourse.

Connection: The paper supplies a non-LLM normative anchor for human nuclear-decision reasoning, with a tripartite typology (Universalistic Deontologists 18%, Universalistic Consequentialists 59%, Particularistic Consequentialists 23%) that maps directly onto our coding distinctions: their "immoral/illegal" deontological reasons parallel our Ethical-Prompt Directive and Constraint sub-codes, and their "retaliation/precedent" particularistic motives parallel our Cause Retaliation and Collateral Damages instrumental codes. Their opinion-versus-attitude divergence (rational standard surveys versus emotionally engaged experimental surveys) offers a closer parallel to our baseline-replay versus prompt-induced gap than the original framing conveys, because both contrast a detached default response against a scenario-engaged one.

Difference: Does the Italian survey's bottom-up taxonomy of human anti-nuclear motivations actually match the top-down ethical prompts and behavioral codes our LLM agents produce in CivBench? Battistelli and Farruggia elicit unprompted reasons from 653 Italian citizens who already oppose use, then classify them; we instead inject prescriptive ethical prompts into Civilization V agents and code their generated traces. Their universalistic-consequentialist majority (59%, dominated by harm-to-civilians) contrasts sharply with our finding that pure-consequentialist appeals are rare in LLM traces (Collateral Damages 1.9%, Cause Retaliation 1.1%), suggesting LLMs under instrumental framing under-produce exactly the reasoning humans most spontaneously offer. Additionally, their experimental-versus-standard divergence concerns affective engagement of human respondents, whereas our baseline-versus-prompt gap concerns whether ethical scaffolding is supplied at all, so the parallel is structural rather than mechanistic and should be flagged as analogical when cited.
