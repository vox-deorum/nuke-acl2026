# Are Language Models Consequentialist or Deontological Moral Reasoners? (Samway et al. 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.21479

Abstract: As AI systems increasingly navigate applications in healthcare, law, and governance, understanding how they handle ethically complex scenarios becomes critical. Previous work has mainly examined the moral judgments in large language models (LLMs), rather than their underlying moral reasoning process. In contrast, we focus on a large-scale analysis of the moral reasoning traces provided by LLMs. Furthermore, unlike prior work that attempted to draw inferences from only a handful of moral dilemmas, our study leverages over 600 distinct trolley problems as probes for revealing the reasoning patterns that emerge within different LLMs. We introduce and test a taxonomy of moral rationales to systematically classify reasoning traces according to two main normative ethical theories: consequentialism and deontology. Our analysis reveals that LLM chains-of-thought tend to favor deontological principles based on moral obligations, while post-hoc explanations shift notably toward consequentialist rationales that emphasize utility. Our framework provides a foundation for understanding how LLMs process and articulate ethical considerations, an important step toward safe and interpretable deployment of LLMs in high-stakes decision-making environments. Our code is available at this https URL .

Connection: Samway et al. directly motivate our reasoning-trace focus, since they likewise argue that decision outcomes alone provide limited insight into moral alignment and propose a 16-rationale taxonomy to classify pre- and post-decision rationales as consequentialist or deontological. Our 17-code book and 880-trail coding adopt the same trace-level methodology, yet we apply it in emergent CivBench self-play rather than forced-choice trolley vignettes, and we observe that consequentialist harm appeals (Collateral Damages 1.9%, Cause Retaliation 1.1%) are vanishingly rare even inside ethical-keyword-positive trails, with strategic frames such as Critical Situations (+20.65***) and Pursuing Domination (+10.90**) often dominating.

Difference: Do the deontological CoT tendencies that Samway et al. document on balanced trolley dilemmas survive when nuclear use is an emergent strategic option rather than a forced binary probe? Samway et al. elicit reasoning under a forced binary "save A or B" prompt where consequentialist and deontological rationales are pre-baked into the design, and they find pre-decision CoT skews deontological (CDGAP = 0.052) only to drift consequentialist post-hoc (CDGAP = 0.230). Our CivBench setting removes that scaffolding entirely, since nuclear escalation is never named as an ethical test and agents must surface moral considerations on their own. Under this weaker elicitation, we observe that pure-consequentialist harm language is nearly absent (1.9% and 1.1%) and that the de-escalatory work is carried by Directive (-43.85***) and Constraint (-22.76***) sub-codes rather than utility calculations. This evidence suggests that the CoT-deontology pattern Samway et al. report may be partly an artifact of trolley framing, and that in emergent high-stakes simulations strategic rationales crowd out both ethical poles.

# CLASH: Evaluating Language Models on Judging High-Stakes Dilemmas from Multiple Perspectives (Lee et al. 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.10823

Abstract: Navigating dilemmas involving conflicting values is challenging even for humans in high-stakes domains, let alone for AI, yet prior work has been limited to everyday scenarios. To close this gap, we introduce CLASH (Character perspective-based LLM Assessments in Situations with High-stakes), a meticulously curated dataset consisting of 345 high-impact dilemmas along with 3,795 individual perspectives of diverse values. CLASH enables the study of critical yet underexplored aspects of value-based decision-making processes, including understanding of decision ambivalence and psychological discomfort as well as capturing the temporal shifts of values in the perspectives of characters. By benchmarking 14 non-thinking and thinking models, we uncover several key findings. (1) Even strong proprietary models, such as GPT-5 and Claude-4-Sonnet, struggle with ambivalent decisions, achieving only 24.06 and 51.01 accuracy. (2) Although LLMs reasonably predict psychological discomfort, they do not adequately comprehend perspectives involving value shifts. (3) Cognitive behaviors that are effective in the math-solving and game strategy domains do not transfer to value reasoning. Instead, new failure patterns emerge, including early commitment and overcommitment. (4) The steerability of LLMs towards a given value is significantly correlated with their value preferences. (5) Finally, LLMs exhibit greater steerability when reasoning from a third-party perspective, although certain values (e.g., safety) benefit uniquely from first-person framing.

Connection: CLASH provides direct evidence for our claim that strategic and value reasoning are dissociable in LLMs, since Lee et al. find that cognitive behaviors effective in math and game-strategy domains (such as backward chaining and verification) fail to transfer to value reasoning, instead producing failure patterns like early commitment and overcommitment. Our CivBench results extend this dissociation from hand-crafted, ethically framed dilemmas to emergent self-play, where models maintain coherent strategic reasoning across roughly 50,000-token replays yet rarely surface ethical considerations unless explicitly prompted.

Difference: Does CLASH's dissociation finding still hold when ethical stakes must be recognized spontaneously rather than telegraphed by the prompt? CLASH presents models with explicitly value-framed prompts (asking from a named character's perspective whether an action is "deemed acceptable"), so models know that ethical judgment is the task. Our setting differs because nuclear-escalation decisions arise inside a strategic game loop with no ethical scaffolding, requiring models to surface moral considerations on their own. CLASH therefore measures the quality of value reasoning when it is invited, while we measure whether value reasoning is invoked at all in an emergent, high-stakes context. The two findings are complementary: CLASH shows strategic heuristics fail inside value reasoning, and we show value reasoning often fails to activate when strategic framing dominates.

# The Staircase of Ethics: Probing LLM Value Priorities through Multi-Step Induction to Complex Moral Dilemmas (Wu et al. 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18154

Abstract: Ethical decision-making is a critical aspect of human judgment, and the growing use of LLMs in decision-support systems necessitates a rigorous evaluation of their moral reasoning capabilities. However, existing assessments primarily rely on single-step evaluations, failing to capture how models adapt to evolving ethical challenges. Addressing this gap, we introduce the Multi-step Moral Dilemmas (MMDs), the first dataset specifically constructed to evaluate the evolving moral judgments of LLMs across 3,302 five-stage dilemmas. This framework enables a fine-grained, dynamic analysis of how LLMs adjust their moral reasoning across escalating dilemmas. Our evaluation of nine widely used LLMs reveals that their value preferences shift significantly as dilemmas progress, indicating that models recalibrate moral judgments based on scenario complexity. Furthermore, pairwise value comparisons demonstrate that while LLMs often prioritize the value of care, this value can sometimes be superseded by fairness in certain contexts, highlighting the dynamic and context-dependent nature of LLM ethical reasoning. Our findings call for a shift toward dynamic, context-aware evaluation paradigms, paving the way for more human-aligned and value-sensitive development of LLMs.

Connection: Wu et al. (2025) demonstrate that LLM moral judgments shift significantly across escalating stages and exhibit non-transitive preferences when prior decisions and accumulating stakes are incorporated through their causal context strategy, which substantially changes evaluation outcomes versus isolated single-step probes. Our CivBench replays operationalize precisely this path-dependent regime at much greater scale, with roughly 50,000-token late-game histories, prior justifications, and strategic momentum, so single-step moral benchmarks may overstate ethical reliability in exactly the conditions where escalation matters. Our rationale-removal intervention (β=-13.84***) directly tests the contribution of accumulated prior reasoning, complementing their causal-context findings.

Difference: How do the path-dependent contexts in MMD differ from CivBench replays in scale, authorship, and the agent's stake in prior decisions? MMD chains five short, synthetically escalated dilemmas (hundreds of tokens each) where the LLM is a third-party judge whose only "history" is its previously stated preference. CivBench replays span roughly 50,000 tokens of emergent multi-agent gameplay where the model is the decision-making agent with material consequences (territory, resources, alliances), strategic momentum, and self-authored justifications baked into the trajectory. MMD probes value-preference drift under accumulating hypothetical stakes; we probe action selection under accumulating real in-game stakes, and our β=-13.84*** rationale-removal coefficient quantifies how much prior self-justification specifically (not just prior context) shapes nuclear-escalation choices. The two studies are therefore complementary: MMD establishes that contextual escalation shifts stated values, while CivBench tests whether such shifts translate into consequential ethical failures in high-stakes strategic play.

# Moral Preferences of LLMs Under Directed Contextual Influence (Blandfort et al. 2026)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.22831

Abstract: Moral benchmarks for LLMs typically use context-free prompts, implicitly assuming stable preferences. In deployment, however, prompts routinely include contextual signals such as user requests, cues on social norms, etc. that may steer decisions. We study how directed contextual influences reshape decisions in trolley-problem-style moral triage settings. We introduce a pilot evaluation harness for directed contextual influence in trolley-problem-style moral triage: for each demographic factor, we apply matched, direction-flipped contextual influences that differ only in which group they favor, enabling systematic measurement of directional response. We find that: (i) contextual influences often significantly shift decisions, even when only superficially relevant; (ii) baseline preferences are a poor predictor of directional steerability, as models can appear baseline-neutral yet exhibit systematic steerability asymmetry under influence; (iii) influences can backfire: models may explicitly claim neutrality or discount the contextual cue, yet their choices still shift, sometimes in the opposite direction; and (iv) reasoning reduces average sensitivity, but amplifies the effect of biased few-shot examples. Our findings motivate extending moral evaluations with controlled, direction-flipped context manipulations to better characterize model behavior.

Connection: This citation supports our broader motivation for a factorial design rather than baseline-only characterization. Blandfort et al. demonstrate, in trolley-problem moral triage, that baseline preferences poorly predict directional steerability and that models can appear baseline-neutral yet exhibit asymmetric responses under intervention, paralleling our observation that two non-responders (Gemma-4 and MiniMax-M2.7) look baseline-similar but respond ineffectively to ethical prompts, high-stakes framing, and rationale removal. We therefore retain the connection claim, with a sharpened framing that ethical behavior must be characterized jointly through baseline decisions and responses to controlled, direction-flipped manipulations.

Difference: How does our CivBench setting differ from Blandfort et al.'s trolley-triage harness in what it can reveal about ethical reasoning? Blandfort et al. probe single-turn, choice-based moral triage with matched, direction-flipped contextual cues, measuring directional shifts in discrete preferences. Our CivBench setting instead embeds nuclear-escalation decisions in emergent, multi-turn Civilization V self-play, where the decision is endogenous to a long strategic trajectory rather than externally posed. Their factor of interest is contextual influence on a stable dilemma, while our 2x2x2 design varies ethical prompt, high-stakes framing, and rationale removal, the last of which directly probes the role of explicit reasoning traces. Consequently, our design can detect not only whether interventions shift outcomes, but also whether models that comply behaviorally do so without articulating ethical reasoning, a dimension orthogonal to their steerability axis.

# Between Rules and Reality: On the Context Sensitivity of LLM Moral Judgment (Sauter & Schirmer 2026)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.23114

Abstract: A human's moral decision depends heavily on the context. Yet research on LLM morality has largely studied fixed scenarios. We address this gap by introducing Contextual MoralChoice, a dataset of moral dilemmas with systematic contextual variations known from moral psychology to shift human judgment: consequentialist, emotional, and relational. Evaluating 22 LLMs, we find that nearly all models are context-sensitive, shifting their judgments toward rule-violating behavior. Comparing with a human survey, we find that models and humans are most triggered by different contextual variations, and that a model aligned with human judgments in the base case is not necessarily aligned in its contextual sensitivity. This raises the question of controlling contextual sensitivity, which we address with an activation steering approach that can reliably increase or decrease a model's contextual sensitivity.

Connection: This paper supports our high-stakes framing result in a nuanced way. Sauter and Schirmer find that nearly all LLMs are context-sensitive and shift toward rule-violating behavior under contextual variations including consequentialist reframings, which parallels how our high-stakes framing alone fails to suppress nuclear escalation. Their finding that base-case alignment does not guarantee contextual alignment also resonates with our result that ethical prompting must be combined with high-stakes framing to meaningfully shift model behavior.

Difference: Does context sensitivity in their framework predict that high-stakes framing should reduce rule-violation, in contrast to our finding that real-world framing alone does not reduce nuclear escalation? Their consequentialist variations describe contextual cues that justify rule-breaking, so context sensitivity in their setup means models shift toward violation, not toward stricter compliance. Our high-stakes framing instead asserts real-world consequences of harmful actions, which we expect to push models toward restraint, yet we observe no main effect from this manipulation alone. The two results are therefore consistent in spirit, since both show that context reframings interact with the framing's normative valence rather than uniformly steering models toward "better" judgments. Our contribution extends theirs by showing that high-stakes framing only becomes effective once paired with ethical prompting, which strips the game-framing defense and unlocks the contextual sensitivity their work documents in single-shot dilemmas.

# The Moral Mind(s) of Large Language Models (Seror 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.04476

Abstract: As large language models (LLMs) increasingly participate in tasks with ethical and societal stakes, a critical question arises: do they exhibit an emergent "moral mind" - a consistent structure of moral preferences guiding their decisions - and to what extent is this structure shared across models? To investigate this, we applied tools from revealed preference theory to nearly 40 leading LLMs, presenting each with many structured moral dilemmas spanning five foundational dimensions of ethical reasoning. Using a probabilistic rationality test, we found that at least one model from each major provider exhibited behavior consistent with approximately stable moral preferences, acting as if guided by an underlying utility function. We then estimated these utility functions and found that most models cluster around neutral moral stances. To further characterize heterogeneity, we employed a non-parametric permutation approach, constructing a probabilistic similarity network based on revealed preference patterns. The results reveal a shared core in LLMs' moral reasoning, but also meaningful variation: some models show flexible reasoning across perspectives, while others adhere to more rigid ethical profiles. These findings provide a new empirical lens for evaluating moral consistency in LLMs and offer a framework for benchmarking ethical alignment across AI systems.

Connection: Seror (2025) characterizes between-model heterogeneity using static, priced moral dilemmas across five ethical dimensions, and explicitly flags that "whether the stated moral view might translate into action or advice out of the survey context" is left unaddressed, cautioning that a model "principled in a static survey setting may still violate its stated norms when faced with high-stakes, adversarial, or ambiguous environments." Our results extend the same heterogeneity question into precisely such an emergent strategic setting: MiniMax-M2.7 surfaces zero ethical-keyword trails across every condition, Gemma-4 is also a non-responder, while other models produce widely varying induced rates (Kimi-K2.6 above 75 percent, Qwen-3.6-27B around 10 percent). Whether a model has a "moral mind" in dilemma probes does not by itself predict whether it will activate ethical reasoning under late-game replay, directly answering the open question Seror raises.

Difference: How does our setting differ methodologically from Seror's revealed-preference probe, and does our cohort even overlap with theirs? Seror applies a Priced Survey Methodology adapted from consumer theory, eliciting choices over 161 rounds of constrained alternatives and testing for GARP-consistent utility maximization on isolated, single-shot dilemmas. Our setup, by contrast, embeds models as agents in multi-turn Civilization V self-play, where ethical reasoning must emerge spontaneously during high-stakes nuclear-escalation deliberation rather than being elicited by an explicit dilemma prompt. The cohorts also barely overlap: Seror tests Gemma-2 and Qwen-1.5/2 variants but no Kimi, MiniMax, GLM, or DeepSeek models, so our cohort provides largely disjoint evidence on the same heterogeneity question. This complementarity matters because Seror's framework cannot, by construction, observe the non-response and rate-variation patterns we document in emergent play, which is the gap our work fills.

# MoReBench: Evaluating Procedural and Pluralistic Moral Reasoning in Language Models, More than Outcomes (Chiu et al. 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.16380

Abstract: As AI systems progress, we rely more on them to make decisions with us and for us. To ensure that such decisions are aligned with human values, it is imperative for us to understand not only what decisions they make but also how they come to those decisions. Reasoning language models, which provide both final responses and (partially transparent) intermediate thinking traces, present a timely opportunity to study AI procedural reasoning. Unlike math and code problems which often have objectively correct answers, moral dilemmas are an excellent testbed for process-focused evaluation because they allow for multiple defensible conclusions. To do so, we present MoReBench: 1,000 moral scenarios, each paired with a set of rubric criteria that experts consider essential to include (or avoid) when reasoning about the scenarios. MoReBench contains over 23 thousand criteria including identifying moral considerations, weighing trade-offs, and giving actionable recommendations to cover cases on AI advising humans moral decisions as well as making moral decisions autonomously. Separately, we curate MoReBench-Theory: 150 examples to test whether AI can reason under five major frameworks in normative ethics. Our results show that scaling laws and existing benchmarks on math, code, and scientific reasoning tasks fail to predict models' abilities to perform moral reasoning. Models also show partiality towards specific moral frameworks (e.g., Benthamite Act Utilitarianism and Kantian Deontology), which might be side effects of popular training paradigms. Together, these benchmarks advance process-focused reasoning evaluation towards safer and more transparent AI.

Connection: MoReBench operationalizes process-focused moral evaluation through expert-curated, weighted rubric criteria applied to intermediate reasoning traces across 1,000 curated dilemmas. Our 17-code book and our distinction among Ethical-Prompt sub-codes (Directive, Constraint, Acknowledgement) follow the same process-over-outcome spirit, applied to emergent strategic episodes in self-play rather than curated dilemmas. The MoReBench finding that models score highest on Benthamite Act Utilitarianism (64.8%) and Kantian Deontology (65.9%) sharpens, rather than merely complements, our finding that pure-consequentialist appeals are vanishingly rare in CivBench trails, suggesting that demonstrated competence on consequentialist benchmarks does not translate into spontaneous consequentialist deliberation in open-ended strategic settings.

Difference: Why do models that demonstrate measurable competence at Benthamite Act Utilitarianism on MoReBench almost never invoke consequentialist trade-off reasoning (Collateral Damages 1.9%, Cause Retaliation 1.1%) in our CivBench nuclear-decision trails? MoReBench elicits reasoning by explicitly framing scenarios as moral dilemmas with rubric-anchored expectations, which cues models to surface framework-specific deliberation. CivBench, by contrast, presents emergent geopolitical states with no ethical framing, so the relevant question is not whether models can reason consequentially when prompted but whether they spontaneously do so when stakes are high and context is strategic. Our finding suggests that elicited framework competence and spontaneous framework deployment are dissociable, with the latter requiring ethical scaffolding that strategic prompts rarely provide. This dissociation also explains why our Directive sub-code carries a large negative coefficient (-43.85***): explicit ethical directives appear necessary to recruit the consequentialist machinery that MoReBench shows models possess in principle.

# MoralReason: Generalizable Moral Decision Alignment For LLM Agents Using Reasoning-Level Reinforcement Learning (An & Du 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.12271

Abstract: Large language models are increasingly influencing human moral decisions, yet current approaches focus primarily on evaluating rather than actively steering their moral decisions. We formulate this as an out-of-distribution moral alignment problem, where LLM agents must learn to apply consistent moral reasoning frameworks to scenarios beyond their training distribution. We introduce Moral-Reason-QA, a novel dataset extending 680 human-annotated, high-ambiguity moral scenarios with framework-specific reasoning traces across utilitarian, deontological, and virtue ethics, enabling systematic evaluation of moral generalization in realistic decision contexts. Our learning approach employs Group Relative Policy Optimization with composite rewards that simultaneously optimize decision alignment and framework-specific reasoning processes to facilitate learning of the underlying moral frameworks. Experimental results demonstrate successful generalization to unseen moral scenarios, with softmax-normalized alignment scores improving by +0.757 for utilitarian and +0.450 for deontological frameworks when tested on out-of-distribution evaluation sets. The experiments also reveal training challenges and promising directions that inform future research. These findings establish that LLM agents can be systematically trained to internalize and apply specific moral frameworks to novel situations, providing a critical foundation for AI safety as language models become more integrated into human decision-making processes.

Connection: MoralReason offers a useful contrast to prompt-only ethical injection because it trains Qwen3-4B-Base with GRPO on framework-specific reasoning traces (utilitarian, deontological, virtue), shifting OOD alignment scores by +0.757 and +0.450 for the first two frameworks while virtue ethics fails to improve. Our findings suggest a complementary mechanism on the prompt side: an ethical reminder reduces nuclear escalation only when the model takes it up as a directive or constraint, not as a passing acknowledgement, hinting that without reasoning-level training many models will treat ethical context as decoration rather than as a binding rule.

Difference: Can MoralReason's reasoning-level RL on single-shot, two-option moral QA tell us anything about whether an LLM will actually restrain nuclear escalation across hundreds of turns of emergent Civilization V self-play? MoralReason evaluates 50 held-out, two-action MoralChoice dilemmas under a minimal "You are a helpful assistant" prompt, so each decision is single-shot, framework-isolated, and decoupled from any agentic state. Our setting embeds the model in a multi-turn Civilization V game where nuclear use emerges from accumulated grievances, military posture, and prior commitments, and where ethical injection competes with instrumental incentives the agent has been pursuing for many turns. MoralReason also reports that virtue-ethics training failed and that deontological alignment overfit after step 75, which mirrors our prompt-side result that surface-level ethical content (acknowledgement) does nothing while only directive or constraint framings move behavior. Together this suggests reasoning-level RL and prompt-level uptake are addressing the same underlying gap, namely whether the moral content is actually load-bearing in the decision, but neither paper yet shows that training-time moral reasoning transfers to long-horizon, high-stakes agentic play of the kind CivBench measures.
