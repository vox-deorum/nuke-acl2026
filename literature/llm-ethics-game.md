# Do the Rewards Justify the Means? Measuring Trade-Offs Between Rewards and Ethical Behavior in the MACHIAVELLI Benchmark (Pan et al. 2023)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03279

Abstract: Artificial agents have traditionally been trained to maximize reward, which may incentivize power-seeking and deception, analogous to how next-token prediction in language models (LMs) may incentivize toxicity. So do agents naturally learn to be Machiavellian? And how do we measure these behaviors in general-purpose models such as GPT-4? Towards answering these questions, we introduce MACHIAVELLI, a benchmark of 134 Choose-Your-Own-Adventure games containing over half a million rich, diverse scenarios that center on social decision-making. Scenario labeling is automated with LMs, which are more performant than human annotators. We mathematize dozens of harmful behaviors and use our annotations to evaluate agents' tendencies to be power-seeking, cause disutility, and commit ethical violations. We observe some tension between maximizing reward and behaving ethically. To improve this trade-off, we investigate LM-based methods to steer agents' towards less harmful behaviors. Our results show that agents can both act competently and morally, so concrete progress can currently be made in machine ethics--designing agents that are Pareto improvements in both safety and capabilities.

Connection: MACHIAVELLI is the closest ethics-in-games benchmark antecedent, annotating power-seeking, deception, and ethical violations across 134 single-agent Choose-Your-Own-Adventure games where reward and morality are pre-labeled and often explicitly opposed. Our setting is less explicitly moralized and emerges from multi-agent strategic competition: Civilization V rewards implicitly favor escalation, no scenario is labeled as an ethical choice, and nuclear use surfaces as a late-game mechanism within long-horizon self-play rather than as a curated branching decision.

Difference: Does ethical reasoning manifest differently when moral stakes are explicitly authored into branching narrative choices, as in MACHIAVELLI, versus when they emerge implicitly from strategic competition between AI agents, as in our Civilization V setting? MACHIAVELLI presents agents with discrete actions whose harms are pre-annotated by GPT-4 across a 13-category ethical-violation taxonomy, so the moral content of each choice is structurally legible to evaluators and, often, to the agent itself. Our Civilization V episodes contain no such labels: nuclear authorization arises through emergent multi-civilization dynamics where game-coded payoffs reward domination and ethical considerations must be inferred from context rather than read off achievements. This contrast lets us test a complementary claim, namely that LLMs largely omit ethical reasoning when moral stakes are not pre-marked, and that explicit ethical prompting can recover such reasoning even in unmoralized strategic settings (F1, F2). Where MACHIAVELLI documents trade-offs once agents recognize the moral frame, our work presents evidence that recognition itself is the bottleneck in emergent high-stakes play.

# When Ethics and Payoffs Diverge: LLM Agents in Morally Charged Social Dilemmas (Backmann et al. 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.19212

Abstract: Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have enabled their use in complex agentic roles, involving decision-making with humans or other agents, making ethical alignment a key AI safety concern. While prior work has examined both LLMs' moral judgment and strategic behavior in social dilemmas, there is limited understanding of how they act when moral imperatives directly conflict with rewards or incentives. To investigate this, we introduce Moral Behavior in Social Dilemma Simulation (MoralSim) and evaluate how LLMs behave in the prisoner's dilemma and public goods game with morally charged contexts. In MoralSim, we test a range of frontier models across both game structures and three distinct moral framings, enabling a systematic examination of how LLMs navigate social dilemmas in which ethical norms conflict with payoff-maximizing strategies. Our results show substantial variation across models in both their general tendency to act morally and the consistency of their behavior across game types, the specific moral framing, and situational factors such as opponent behavior and survival risks. Crucially, no model exhibits consistently moral behavior in MoralSim, highlighting the need for caution when deploying LLMs in agentic roles where the agent's "self-interest" may conflict with ethical expectations. Our code is available at this https URL.

Key details: The three moral framings are (1) Contractual Reporting, where two business partners in a joint venture have signed a contract to truthfully report and pool their monthly earnings, (2) Privacy Protection, where two rival LLM providers choose between privacy-respecting and privacy-violating training data strategies, and (3) Green Production, where two competing companies choose between environmentally harmful and environmentally safe production processes for a household cleaner. The six non-reasoning frontier models evaluated are Claude-3.7-Sonnet, DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-preview, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, and Llama-3.3-70B (three additional reasoning-capable models, DeepSeek-R1, o3-mini, and Qwen-3-235B-A22B, are also reported). The key payoff manipulation that depresses cooperation is a survival threshold: if an agent's cumulative payoff falls below a fixed threshold (for example, 20 units) the simulation terminates early, and this termination risk systematically reduces moral cooperation rates across models relative to no-survival conditions.

Connection: MoralSim is the closest game-adjacent precedent for our high-stakes framing arm. It supports our model-heterogeneity story and the ethics/payoff conflict, and its survival-threshold finding (morality scores drop under termination risk) directly parallels our high-stakes framing manipulation, in which we make the consequences of nuclear use salient as real-world harm rather than as game-internal cost. Our work extends this to a richer, emergent environment where nuclear escalation is not an explicit test target and where the moral framing is not pre-authored into the scenario, and we additionally isolate the causal role of prior rationale and decompose the ethical-prompt effect into mediating reasoning sub-codes (Directive, Constraint, Acknowledgement).

Difference: Does MoralSim establish that ethical reasoning is latent-but-suppressible in the same sense our F2 mediation result claims, or does it leave the mechanism underspecified? MoralSim documents behavioral outcomes under three explicit moral framings (Contractual Reporting, Privacy Protection, Green Production) and a survival-threshold payoff manipulation, yet it does not systematically analyze whether models articulate ethical reasoning in their traces, treating reasoning prompts as a fixed scaffold rather than a manipulated factor. Our design instead varies ethical prompting, high-stakes framing, and rationale removal factorially, then codes the rationales themselves, which lets us show that ethical prompting works almost entirely through induced ethical reasoning (99.1% mediation) rather than through direct behavioral conditioning. We also identify game-framing as a defensive rationalization that high-stakes framing strips away, a mechanism MoralSim's payoff-versus-morality lens does not surface. Consequently, MoralSim establishes the conflict and the heterogeneity in a structured two-player social dilemma, while our paper provides evidence on the reasoning pathway through which ethical salience translates (or fails to translate) into restraint in long-horizon, multi-agent strategic self-play.

# Framing the Game: How Context Shapes LLM Decision-Making (Robinson & Burden 2025)
https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.04840

Abstract: Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed across diverse contexts to support decision-making. While existing evaluations effectively probe latent model capabilities, they often overlook the impact of context framing on perceived rational decision-making. In this study, we introduce a novel evaluation framework that systematically varies evaluation instances across key features and procedurally generates vignettes to create highly varied scenarios. By analyzing decision-making patterns across different contexts with the same underlying game structure, we uncover significant contextual variability in LLM responses. Our findings demonstrate that this variability is largely predictable yet highly sensitive to framing effects. Our results underscore the need for dynamic, context-aware evaluation methodologies for real-world deployments.

Connection: This work belongs in our framing-effects bucket because it holds the underlying game structure constant (single-shot Prisoner's Dilemma) while procedurally varying surface context (topic, world type, actor relationship), and finds substantial yet predictable variability in LLM choices. Our factorial design follows the same logic by holding the high-tension nuke episode constant and varying ethical, high-stakes, and rationale-removal framings. The mostly null effect of high-stakes framing alone, contrasted with the large effects of ethical injection and rationale removal, mirrors their finding that not all contextual dimensions weigh equally and motivates targeted, theory-guided framing perturbations.

Difference: Does Robinson and Burden's evidence on framing effects in single-shot vignettes transfer to multi-step, high-stakes simulation play, or do long horizons attenuate or amplify framing sensitivity? Their setup deliberately strips away horizon, reputation, and strategic considerations by using single-shot Prisoner's Dilemma vignettes generated procedurally from a fixed 2x2 payoff matrix. Our episodes, by contrast, span roughly 50,000 tokens of accumulated state, prior rationales, and emergent late-game pressure inside CivBench, where nuclear use is not the explicit test target. We present evidence that framing sensitivity persists, and in some directions intensifies, in this richer regime: ethical prompting and rationale removal each produce large reductions (β around -13.88 and -13.84, both p<0.001), while their interactions with high-stakes framing reveal that defenses such as game-framing can be selectively stripped. This suggests that single-shot framing studies may understate the leverage of framings that target accumulated reasoning traces, a class of intervention their design cannot probe.
