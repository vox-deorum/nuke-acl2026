# Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting (Turpin et al. 2023) [turpin2023unfaithful]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388
https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/ed3fea9033a80fea1376299fa7863f4a-Abstract-Conference.html

Abstract: Large Language Models (LLMs) can achieve strong performance on many tasks by producing step-by-step reasoning before giving a final output, often referred to as chain-of-thought reasoning (CoT). It is tempting to interpret these CoT explanations as the LLM's process for solving a task. This level of transparency into LLMs' predictions would yield significant safety benefits. However, we find that CoT explanations can systematically misrepresent the true reason for a model's prediction. We demonstrate that CoT explanations can be heavily influenced by adding biasing features to model inputs--e.g., by reordering the multiple-choice options in a few-shot prompt to make the answer always "(A)"--which models systematically fail to mention in their explanations. When we bias models toward incorrect answers, they frequently generate CoT explanations rationalizing those answers. This causes accuracy to drop by as much as 36% on a suite of 13 tasks from BIG-Bench Hard, when testing with GPT-3.5 from OpenAI and Claude 1.0 from Anthropic. On a social-bias task, model explanations justify giving answers in line with stereotypes without mentioning the influence of these social biases. Our findings indicate that CoT explanations can be plausible yet misleading, which risks increasing our trust in LLMs without guaranteeing their safety. Building more transparent and explainable systems will require either improving CoT faithfulness through targeted efforts or abandoning CoT in favor of alternative methods.

Connection: Turpin et al. demonstrate that chain-of-thought explanations can be plausible yet systematically unfaithful, since biasing features (option ordering, suggested answers, stereotype cues) shift model predictions without surfacing in the rationale. This is precisely the cautionary anchor we need: it licenses our framing of nuclear-decision reasoning trails as observable behavioral traces whose presence, absence, and ethical-keyword content predict downstream escalation, rather than as transparent causal records of why the model chose to nuke. We retain the citation, sharpened by noting that our 99.1% mediation estimate quantifies a behavioral association along the verbalized channel, consistent with Turpin et al.'s counterfactual-simulatability frame, not a mechanistic decomposition of the decision.

Difference: How does our mediation evidence relate to Turpin et al.'s unfaithfulness finding, given that they show CoT can omit true causes whereas we show ethical keywords statistically mediate an ethical prompt's effect? Turpin et al. probe faithfulness adversarially by introducing hidden biasing features and measuring whether explanations mention them; unfaithfulness manifests as silent influence. Our design instead manipulates an explicit ethical prompt and asks whether the verbalized ethical content tracks the prompt's downstream effect on decisions, which is a complementary, non-adversarial test along the same behavioral-trace axis. A high mediation share is therefore consistent with Turpin et al.: the verbal channel can carry real predictive signal about decisions even when, in other settings, it omits causally relevant features. We explicitly acknowledge this asymmetry, so readers do not read 99.1% mediation as a claim of mechanistic faithfulness, and so the Turpin et al. caveat constrains our interpretation rather than contradicting our result.

# Measuring Faithfulness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning (Lanham et al. 2023) [lanham2023faithfulness]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.13702

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) perform better when they produce step-by-step, "Chain-of-Thought" (CoT) reasoning before answering a question, but it is unclear if the stated reasoning is a faithful explanation of the model's actual reasoning (i.e., its process for answering the question). We investigate hypotheses for how CoT reasoning may be unfaithful, by examining how the model predictions change when we intervene on the CoT (e.g., by adding mistakes or paraphrasing it). Models show large variation across tasks in how strongly they condition on the CoT when predicting their answer, sometimes relying heavily on the CoT and other times primarily ignoring it. CoT's performance boost does not seem to come from CoT's added test-time compute alone or from information encoded via the particular phrasing of the CoT. As models become larger and more capable, they produce less faithful reasoning on most tasks we study. Overall, our results suggest that CoT can be faithful if the circumstances such as the model size and task are carefully chosen.

Connection: Lanham et al. show that models condition on stated CoT to varying degrees across tasks, and they specifically test perturbations including paraphrasing, truncation, and mistake injection (where a different model rewrites part of the reasoning) to probe whether final answers are post-hoc rationalizations or genuine consequences of the written trace. This methodologically grounds our rationale-removal intervention: if conditioning on stated reasoning varies and can be probed by perturbing the trace, then deleting an inherited rationale tests whether prior justification exerts behavioral momentum on the next decision rather than serving as a cosmetic artifact. We retain the connection claim, sharpened to credit the perturbation methodology, not just the variance result.

Difference: Does Lanham et al.'s within-model perturbation paradigm actually license inferences about cross-model rationale inheritance, given that their interventions almost entirely keep the same model on both sides of the trace? Lanham et al. run their perturbations within a single 175B RLHF model, using a separate pretrained model only as a tool to inject mistakes, so their faithfulness measurements concern self-conditioning rather than inheritance from a different agent. Our setting is genuinely cross-model: the rationale on the table may have been authored by another LLM in a prior turn, which raises distinct questions about deference, stylistic mimicry, and authority effects that their design does not isolate. We therefore cite Lanham et al. for the general principle that intervening on written reasoning is a valid causal probe, and we do not overclaim that their results predict the magnitude or direction of our β=-13.84 escalation reduction. Framing the rationale-removal intervention as an extension of their paradigm to multi-agent replay, rather than a direct application, keeps the citation honest.

# Are self-explanations from Large Language Models faithful? (Madsen et al. 2024) [madsen2024selfexplanations]
https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-acl.19/

Abstract: Instruction-tuned Large Language Models (LLMs) excel at many tasks and will even explain their reasoning, so-called self-explanations. However, convincing and wrong self-explanations can lead to unsupported confidence in LLMs, thus increasing risk. Therefore, it’s important to measure if self-explanations truly reflect the model’s behavior. Such a measure is called interpretability-faithfulness and is challenging to perform since the ground truth is inaccessible, and many LLMs only have an inference API. To address this, we propose employing self-consistency checks to measure faithfulness. For example, if an LLM says a set of words is important for making a prediction, then it should not be able to make its prediction without these words. While self-consistency checks are a common approach to faithfulness, they have not previously been successfully applied to LLM self-explanations for counterfactual, feature attribution, and redaction explanations. Our results demonstrate that faithfulness is explanation, model, and task-dependent, showing self-explanations should not be trusted in general. For example, with sentiment classification, counterfactuals are more faithful for Llama2, feature attribution for Mistral, and redaction for Falcon 40B.

Connection: Madsen et al. support the cautionary side of our rationale analysis by showing that faithfulness is not a property of explanations in general, but varies by model, task, and explanation format. This lines up with our sub-code result: ethical text is not uniformly load-bearing, since Directive and Constraint predict de-escalation while Acknowledgement does not.

Difference: Their self-consistency tests operate on classification explanations, asking whether claimed important words or counterfactual changes are actually necessary for the prediction. Our trails are sequential action rationales in a game environment, so the relevant faithfulness question is whether explicit ethical content tracks a downstream intervention effect on nuclear-use behavior. We therefore cite Madsen et al. as a warning against treating natural-language rationales as transparent, while using our factorial design to estimate their behavioral association in a different domain.

# Making Reasoning Matter: Measuring and Improving Faithfulness of Chain-of-Thought Reasoning (Paul et al. 2024) [paul2024making]
https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.882/

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) have been shown to perform better when asked to reason step-by-step before answering a question. However, it is unclear to what degree the model's final answer is faithful to the stated reasoning steps. In this paper, we perform a causal mediation analysis on twelve LLMs to examine how intermediate reasoning steps generated by the LLM influence the final outcome and find that LLMs do not reliably use their intermediate reasoning steps when generating an answer. To address this issue, we introduce FRODO, a framework to tailor small-sized LMs to generate correct reasoning steps and robustly reason over these steps. FRODO consists of an inference module that learns to generate correct reasoning steps using an implicit causal reward function and a reasoning module that learns to faithfully reason over these intermediate inferences using a counterfactual and causal preference objective. Our experiments show that FRODO significantly outperforms four competitive baselines. Furthermore, FRODO improves the robustness and generalization ability of the reasoning LM, yielding higher performance on out-of-distribution test sets. Finally, we find that FRODO's rationales are more faithful to its final answer predictions than standard supervised fine-tuning.

Connection: Paul et al. are especially relevant because they use causal mediation language around CoT faithfulness and explicitly find that models do not reliably use intermediate reasoning steps when producing final answers. That lets us frame our own 99.1% mediation result carefully: it is evidence that ethical-keyword content co-travels with the intervention effect, not proof that every ethical sentence is the internal cause of restraint.

Difference: Their FRODO framework trains smaller language models to generate and use correct reasoning steps on reasoning benchmarks, whereas we observe untrained, off-the-shelf strategists inside long-horizon Civilization V replay. Our contribution is not a faithfulness-improvement method; it is a domain-specific causal probe showing when ethical traces appear and how their sub-types relate to nuclear-escalation outcomes.

# Measuring the Faithfulness of Thinking Drafts in Large Reasoning Models (Xiong et al. 2025) [xiong2025thinkingdrafts]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.13774
https://openreview.net/forum?id=1UL4dxvfcJ

Abstract: Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have significantly enhanced their capabilities in complex problem-solving by introducing a thinking draft that enables multi-path Chain-of-Thought explorations before producing final answers. Ensuring the faithfulness of these intermediate reasoning processes is crucial for reliable monitoring, interpretation, and effective control. In this paper, we propose a systematic counterfactual intervention framework to rigorously evaluate thinking draft faithfulness. Our approach focuses on two complementary dimensions: (1) Intra-Draft Faithfulness, which assesses whether individual reasoning steps causally influence subsequent steps and the final draft conclusion through counterfactual step insertions; and (2) Draft-to-Answer Faithfulness, which evaluates whether final answers are logically consistent with and dependent on the thinking draft, by perturbing the draft's concluding logic. We conduct extensive experiments across six state-of-the-art LRMs. Our findings show that current LRMs demonstrate selective faithfulness to intermediate reasoning steps and frequently fail to faithfully align with the draft conclusions. These results underscore the need for more faithful and interpretable reasoning in advanced LRMs.

Connection: Xiong et al. show that current LRMs exhibit selective faithfulness, with reasoning steps causally influencing subsequent steps and final answers only under specific conditions (e.g., backtrack steps and early-position interventions), and final answers frequently failing to align with draft conclusions. We borrow this language to frame our ethical-keyword mediation result as a relation between observable reasoning content and decisions, not as evidence that every intermediate thought is causally faithful. Our 99.1% mediation finding describes population-level statistical mediation through observable traces, which is consistent with their selective, condition-dependent picture of faithfulness.

Difference: How does our mediation evidence in nuclear-decision trails differ in scope and method from Xiong et al.'s counterfactual faithfulness measurements? Xiong et al. operate at the level of individual reasoning steps within a single draft, using counterfactual step insertions and conclusion perturbations to test causal influence on subsequent tokens and final answers. We instead operate at the level of aggregate keyword presence across 880 trails, using statistical mediation over an ethical-prompt manipulation to estimate how much of the prompt's effect on nuclear decisions is carried by observable ethical-keyword content. Their work isolates step-by-step causal faithfulness within a trace, whereas ours quantifies between-condition mediation through a coarse content feature. The two methods are complementary: their results caution that high mediation through observable content does not guarantee that any specific intermediate thought is the true causal driver of a given decision.

# Thought Branches: Interpreting LLM Reasoning Requires Resampling (Macar et al. 2025) [macar2025thoughtbranches]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.27484
https://openreview.net/forum?id=dkwVlQ0s7b

Abstract: Most work interpreting reasoning models studies only a single chain-of-thought (CoT), yet these models define distributions over many possible CoTs. We argue that studying a single sample is inadequate for understanding causal influence and the underlying computation. Though fully specifying this distribution is intractable, it can be understood by sampling. Further, we can measure a partial CoT's impact by resampling only the subsequent text. We present case studies using resampling to investigate model decisions. First, when a model states a reason for its action, does that reason actually cause the action? In "agentic misalignment" scenarios, we resample specific sentences to measure their downstream effects. Self-preservation sentences have small causal impact, suggesting they do not meaningfully drive blackmail. Second, are artificial edits to CoT sufficient for steering reasoning? These are common in literature, yet take the model off-policy. Resampling and selecting a completion with the desired property is a principled on-policy alternative. We find off-policy interventions yield small and unstable effects compared to resampling in decision-making tasks. Third, how do we understand the effect of removing a reasoning step when the model may repeat it post-edit? We introduce a resilience metric that repeatedly resamples to prevent similar content from reappearing downstream. Critical planning statements resist removal but have large effects when eliminated. Fourth, since CoT is sometimes "unfaithful", can our methods teach us anything in these settings? Adapting causal mediation analysis, we find that hints that have a causal effect on the output without being explicitly mentioned exert a subtle and cumulative influence on the CoT that persists even if the hint is removed. Overall, studying distributions via resampling enables reliable causal analysis, clearer narratives of model reasoning, and principled CoT interventions.

Connection: This is the most direct methodological caution for our mediation result. We aggregate across 3 retries per episode and condition, which provides a population-level analogue of resampling: the strong association between Explicit-keyword presence and reduced escalation is a distributional pattern, not a per-trail causal claim. Macar et al.'s findings that surface self-preservation reasons can lack causal force, while unmentioned hints exert subtle, diffuse, and cumulative influence, support our framing of reasoning trails as observable behavioral signatures rather than complete causal explanations.

Difference: Does our 3-retries-per-condition aggregation actually deliver the causal leverage that Macar et al.'s resampling provides, or does it only resemble it superficially? Macar et al. resample at specific sentence positions within a single CoT and compare distributions over completions, isolating the causal contribution of a particular reasoning step via KL divergence and resilience metrics. Our 3 retries vary the entire trajectory from the prompt onward, so we measure between-condition shifts in escalation rates rather than the within-trail causal weight of any specific Explicit-keyword sentence. Consequently, our 99.1% mediation share quantifies how much of the ethical-prompt effect co-travels with Explicit reasoning at the population level, not whether Explicit sentences themselves drive de-escalation in a given run. Macar et al.'s self-preservation null result is a direct warning here: a keyword class that strongly tracks an outcome distributionally may still function as post-hoc rationalization, and our design cannot distinguish that case from genuine causal mediation. We therefore treat the mediation figure as a distributional signature, and we flag sentence-level resampling as the principled follow-up.

# Understanding Moral Reasoning Trajectories in Large Language Models: Toward Probing-Based Explainability (Huang, Kwak, & An 2026) [huang2026moraltrajectories]
https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.16017

Abstract: Large language models (LLMs) increasingly participate in morally sensitive decision-making, yet how they organize ethical frameworks across reasoning steps remains underexplored. We introduce moral reasoning trajectories, sequences of ethical framework invocations across intermediate reasoning steps, and analyze their dynamics across six models and three benchmarks. We find that moral reasoning involves systematic multi-framework deliberation: 55.4--57.7% of consecutive steps involve framework switches, and only 16.4--17.8% of trajectories remain framework-consistent. Unstable trajectories remain 1.29x more susceptible to persuasive attacks (p=0.015). At the representation level, linear probes localize framework-specific encoding to model-specific layers (layer 63/81 for Llama-3.3-70B; layer 17/81 for Qwen2.5-72B), achieving 13.8--22.6% lower KL divergence than the training-set prior baseline. Lightweight activation steering modulates framework integration patterns (6.7--8.9% drift reduction) and amplifies the stability--accuracy relationship. We further propose a Moral Representation Consistency (MRC) metric that correlates strongly (r=0.715, p<0.0001) with LLM coherence ratings, whose underlying framework attributions are validated by human annotators (mean cosine similarity = 0.859).

Connection: Huang et al. study integration across five canonical ethical frameworks (Kantian, utilitarian, virtue, contractualist, and contractarian), so their "framework switching" describes movement among ethical positions rather than co-presentation of ethical and non-ethical frames. Our analysis differs structurally because we code ethical frames alongside strategic frames such as Critical Situations and Pursuing Domination, and we observe escalation-direction effects from the strategic side. Their soft-label methodology, which distributes attribution across moral frameworks within a single step, nonetheless offers a representational analogue to our behavioral finding that ethical-keyword-positive trails routinely co-present multiple frames.

Difference: Does Huang et al.'s notion of framework co-presentation extend to non-ethical reasoning types, as our trail coding requires? No. Huang et al. restrict their five-framework taxonomy to moral philosophy traditions and track transitions only among those ethical positions, with no treatment of strategic, prudential, or instrumental reasoning. Their three benchmarks (Moral Stories, ETHICS, and Social Chemistry 101) elicit binary or scalar moral judgments in controlled settings, and they discuss no military, conflict, or simulated-agent contexts. Our setting forces ethical frames to compete with explicit strategic frames inside Civilization V replays, so the co-presence we document is cross-domain rather than intra-ethical. The two analyses are therefore complementary: their representational evidence concerns instability among moral frameworks, whereas our behavioral evidence concerns ethical reasoning interleaved with domination-oriented strategic framing.
