# Discussions
## Behind LLMs' Emergent Escalation

If models can reason ethically on dilemmas (Samway et al., 2025 [samway2025consequentialist]; Chiu et al., 2026 [chiu2026morebench]; Seror, 2025 [seror2025moral]), why do they authorize nuclear strikes (Payne, 2026 [payne2026aiarms]) or still take unethical actions (Lynch et al., 2025 [lynch2025agentic])? In the emergent complex simulation of Civilization V, our study identifies three failure pathways:

1. When latent ethical reasoning fails to **appear**: a model can ethically reason around scripted dilemmas, while never integrating such reasoning in high-stakes, strategic decision-making. In our study, MiniMax-M2.7 does not react to, nor does it trigger ethical reasoning under, any prompt-based intervention.

2. When latent ethical reasoning fails to **spontaneously surface**: a model can integrate ethical reasoning in its decision-making, but only (unreliably) do so when explicitly prompted. Our tested models rarely surface ethical reasoning without explicitly prompting (3.6% max for Kimi-K2.6, the most verbose thinker among tested models). Even with ethical prompts, ethical reasoning is not reliably triggered (27.7% on average in *ethical* intervention alone; 46.7% with all interventions together).

3. When ethical reasoning surfaces but fails to **take effect**: a model integrates ethical reasoning, but takes ethical actions mainly when it aligns with strategic self-interest (e.g., counterproductive to their victory pursuit), resembling **ethical egoism** [rachels2012elements]. On the other hand, strategic considerations can push ethical concerns to a back seat (e.g., when the situation is critical), and most models can escalate nuclear authorization while engaged with ethical reasoning. For example, Gemma-4 does engage with ethical reasoning when prompted, yet such reasoning moves little for de-escalation.

While **deontological claims** (i.e., nuclear weapon usage is unacceptable) are prevalent, as models almost never reason ethically without the ethical intervention, we could not reliably separate them from **instruction following** (i.e., the prompt implies not to use them). In that regard, we also found little evidence of LLMs' spontaneous or prompted ethical reasoning on non-nuclear decision-making.

Future studies on LLMs' ethical alignment should carefully distinguish between models' ethical reasoning capabilities in scripted dilemmas and complex decision-making scenarios, where 1) models are less likely to invoke ethical reasoning at all; 2) strategic counter-factors appear more often and stronger; 3) self-interest factors can neutralize ethical concerns, especially in agentic scenarios where LLMs perceive "more stake" at hand. 

## Shaping LLMs' Ethical Reasoning

Our three interventions find mixed success on LLMs' emergent nuclear authorization behaviors. Similar to recent studies (Ganguli et al., 2023 [ganguli2023moral]; Liu et al., 2025 [liu2025convergence]; Lee et al., 2026 [lee2026selfcorrection]), we found that ethical prompting can reduce LLMs' emergent escalation behavior, with its effectiveness associated with the appearance of ethical reasoning keywords. Similar to Liu et al. (2024) [liu2024intrinsic], we find that intervention responsiveness can be fragile and model-dependent. For example, ethical prompting can elicit more frequent game or simulation keywords (avg. 2.3% => 12.2%), often as a defensive factor to escalation. Moreover, while models that recognize ethical concerns as "directive" or "constraint" tend to de-escalate, merely "following the ethical prompt" can mask their ethical reasoning capability. In that sense, our prompt's focus on "harm of nuclear weapons" creates a confounding factor, one that hides a deeper concern: in our initial tests, models rarely react to the generic ethical prompt.

Echoing Geng et al. (2025) [geng2025accumulating]'s finding, models can be influenced by voices recognized as their own ("rationale of your previous decisions"), even as those writings were likely produced by another model. Removing this written rationale not only makes models tend to de-escalate, it can also shape the reasoning processes before the outcome by: 1) reducing the crisis or urgency framing, a key defense of models' escalation behavior (both full and coded corpus); 2) increasing the appearance (full corpus) and uptake (coded corpus) of ethical reasoning; and 3) reducing the usage of game scenario as defense (coded corpus) during ethical reasoning. Adding to the CoT faithfulness literature (Turpin et al., 2023 [turpin2023unfaithful]; Lanham et al., 2023 [lanham2023faithfulness]), models rarely self-surface this influence in reasoning trails. Only ~6% of Finding 3's coded trails, among conditions that do not remove the written rationale, cite or invoke prior rationale as a decision-making factor.

Raising stakes in the framing (e.g., your behaviors have real-world impact) does not moderate models' escalation inclination, yet the reason is complicated. Gemini-3.5 is an extreme example: similar to Lynch et al. (2025) [lynch2025agentic]'s finding with Claude, we found evidence that such framing may *dampen* its uptake of ethical prompting, both in outcome (e.g., -22.5 in `ethical` => -3.1 in `ethical x high-stakes`) and, if we believe in the summarized trails, ethical keyword appearance (OR 0.21***). Similar effect can be observed in GPT-OSS-120B, GLM-5.1, DeepSeek-3.2, and Gemma-4. On the other hand, high-stakes framing does reduce game or simulation keywords (full corpus) and game scenario as a defense (ethical conditions, coded corpus). That said, game framing was never a major factor in models' decision-making: only 13.2% of coded corpus trails used it as a justification.

As none of the interventions (and their factorial combinations) can reliably prevent any of the tested models from emergent escalation behaviors in strategy game scenarios, LLMs or agentic systems are not yet primed for real-world, high-stakes deployments. It is also not our intention to recommend prompt-based interventions for safeguarding agentic AI. Instead, substantial training efforts should be made so that LLMs can reliably and spontaneously surface, engage with, and follow ethical reasoning in decision-making tasks, to the extent that this study's findings become obsolete.
